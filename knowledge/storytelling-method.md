# Storytelling Method — the attention craft behind every piece of content

The canonical knowledge for holding attention with words: hooks, curiosity, rhythm,
and the narrative moves that keep a viewer, reader, or listener locked in. This is
cross-cutting craft, not a single-channel method. A video script, a social post, an
email, ad copy, a thumbnail line, a spoken pitch — all of them live or die on the
same mechanics, so they all come from here.

Consumers that reference this file:

- [`script-my-video`](../skills/script-my-video/SKILL.md) and
  [`youtube-script-method.md`](youtube-script-method.md) — the biggest user; the
  script craft *is* this file, applied to a beat-structured video.
- [`write-post-copy`](../skills/write-post-copy/SKILL.md) — hooks, contrast, term
  branding, atomic sharability for short written posts.
- [`make-social-post`](../skills/make-social-post/SKILL.md) and
  [`social-post-method.md`](social-post-method.md) — the billboard headline plus
  the hook and contrast engine.

**This file owns the craft. It does not own the framing rule.** How the owner frames
their marketing — positive, pain-led, or any psychology they choose — is the owner's
decision (see [`content-rules.md`](content-rules.md), "Marketing framing is the
owner's choice"). This file documents the full technique at high fidelity, including
techniques that lean on negative or pain framing. The owner's brand voice picks the
surface form; the mechanics below are neutral tools. Where a technique's default
surface form is negative, that is flagged so a positive-only brand can keep the
engine and drop the surface.

When in doubt, this file wins over instinct. Instinct says "say the thing clearly."
The method says "open a loop, hold it, and pay it off" — clarity is necessary but it
is not what keeps someone watching.

## The engine: contrast → curiosity gap → prediction

Everything below is one machine wearing different clothes. Understand the machine and
the frameworks stop being a list to memorise.

**Dopamine is the prediction chemical, not the pleasure chemical.** It fires when the
brain is trying to work out what happens next, not when the reward arrives. A vending
machine is not addictive (same result every time); a slot machine is (you cannot
predict the next pull). The whole craft is engineering that "what happens next?"
state and sustaining it.

**Curiosity comes from a gap** — the distance between what someone expects and what
you say is true. The bigger the gap, the bigger the curiosity.

**Contrast is the bridge that builds the gap.** This is the single most reliable
tool in the file. You lean the audience one way, then turn: *"Most owners think the
price wins the job. It's actually the quote."* No contrast, no gap, no curiosity.

Every framework below is a way of creating, holding, or paying off that gap.

## The dance — "but" and "therefore," never "and then"

All good stories alternate between **context** (the character is on a mission, doing
something) and **conflict** (oh no, a complication). Context opens a little; conflict
opens a loop; context closes it; new conflict opens the next. That back-and-forth is
the dance, and it is what keeps someone watching a two-hour film or a forty-second
clip.

The tactical test (from the South Park writers' rule): between any two beats of your
outline, the connector should be **"therefore"** or **"but"** — never **"and then."**

- ❌ *"We quote the job, **and then** we send it, **and then** we follow up."* — piling
  on detail, flat, no loop. The audience drifts.
- ✅ *"We quote the job, **but** the price is the last thing they read, **therefore**
  the first line has to earn its way to the number."* — each turn opens or resolves a
  loop.

If your draft reads as a list joined by "and then," it has no dance. Go through and
force each seam to be a "but" or a "therefore."

## The four-step addiction loop (the macro engine for a whole piece)

The shape that runs underneath any piece that holds attention start to finish. It
repeats: as one loop closes, the next opens.

| Step | What it does | The move |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Stakes** | Turns the prediction machine on. No reason to care, no prediction, no attention. | A character to root for (can be the viewer or you), something at risk, and urgency. "Personally relevant," not "life or death." |
| **2. The big question** | Gives the brain something to solve. Releases a steady low-grade drip of dopamine. | Load a question with just enough detail to make a prediction, not so much it overwhelms. |
| **3. The head fake** | The payoff — a *prediction error* that spikes dopamine hard. Bigger surprise, bigger spike. | An answer they'd never have predicted but that makes complete sense the instant they hear it. Set it up, then turn against the setup. |
| **4. The re-hook** | Closes one loop and opens the next *in the same breath* — no gap. | Phrases that do both at once: "which would've been great, except…", "but here's the problem with that", "and that's when I realised…". |

**The head fake has to be earned.** A surprise that comes from nowhere is just
confusing. A great head fake is one the audience would never have guessed but
immediately understands. Work out what a normal person should be predicting from what
you've said, then contrast against exactly that.

**The re-hook is about *when*, not just what.** The danger zone is the moment a loop
closes and a section ends — that's where attention leaks. Put the next loop-opener
right there, like a relay baton passed while both runners are moving.

## The dopamine ladder — six levels of a viewer's journey

The retention model: six stages that release increasing dopamine. Climb all six
consistently and you build a Pavlovian response — dopamine fires just from seeing your
name or face.

| Level | Stage | What earns it |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Stimulation** | The first 1–2 seconds. A visual stun gun. | Colour, motion, contrast, brightness *different from what the feed usually shows*. Bottom-up processing catches it in ~200ms, before a single word is heard. |
| **2. Captivation** | Curiosity. The open question lands. | The most curiosity-inducing question you can ask about the topic, mapped to what the viewer already cares about. Ask it outright or imply it with detail. |
| **3. Anticipation** | They start guessing the answer. | Feed details that let them predict; ratchet the loop; then head-fake just before the reveal. The highest dopamine is *just before* the answer. |
| **4. Validation** | You deliver the answer. | Close the loop cleanly with something non-obvious. Education: the actual usable tip. Entertainment: the resolution, not a cliffhanger. An unclosed loop leaves them unsatisfied. |
| **5. Affection** | They start to like the person delivering it. | Warmth, a smile (joy transfers through a lens), vibe, and — strongest for education — actually solving their problem. Likability buys a longer leash. |
| **6. Revelation** | They realise you're a repeat source of value. | Deliver non-obvious value again and again until "you" = "value." This is the Pavlovian peak. Education climbs here most easily. |

Faceless content struggles to pass level 5 — there's no one to take a liking to.

## Hooks — the most important lines you write

If they churn on the hook, nothing else you did matters. Three tools, in order of
power.

### The three-step hook formula

1. **Context lean-in** (line 1–2): do two things at once.
   - *Topic clarity* — be immediately clear what this is about, so the right person
     self-selects in and the wrong person self-selects out. Don't trick people into
     watching; you want the ones who care.
   - *The lean* — give them a reason to lean in: common ground, a benefit or a pain
     point they have, a simplifying metaphor, or something so interesting it surprises
     them.
2. **Scroll-stop interjection** (one line): a stun-gun that stops the lean dead. Built
   on a contrast word — "but," "however," "yet," "here's the thing." It's the setup
   for the punch, not the punch itself.
3. **Contrarian snapback** (the punch): turn in the opposite direction of the lean.
   Still on topic, but it snaps them onto a different path. Bigger shock, bigger snap.

> **Service-business example.** *"Most owners think a faster quote wins more jobs.
> [context lean-in] But speed is the least important thing about a quote. [scroll-stop]
> The thing that actually wins it is the one question you ask before you quote at all.
> [snapback]"*

### The six hook power words

A hook can be built from six stackable pieces. Four are core, two optional.

| Piece | Role | In the example |
|---|---|---|
| **Subject clarity** | Who/what the line is about (I, we, you, an entity) | *you* |
| **Action** | What the subject does | *quote* |
| **Objective / end state** | The result. More shocking = better. | *win a $12k job* |
| **Contrast** | New state vs base state — intensifies A→B curiosity | *from a one-line text* |
| **Proof** (optional) | Why your take is worth hearing | *"again"* implies you've done it |
| **Time** (optional) | A constraint that maps urgency/speed | *"in one visit"* |

> *"How I quoted a $12,000 job from a one-line text — in a single visit."* Subject
> (I), action (quote), objective ($12k job), contrast (from a one-line text), time
> (single visit).

**How to practise:** find top accounts in the niche, take their best-performing hooks
from the transcript, identify the six buckets, then swap in your own subject / action
/ objective and re-phrase. Do 10–30 a week. This is copy work — writing a good
writer's words trains the rhythm into your own brain.

### Visual hooks (roughly 10× stronger than words alone)

Eyes read faster than ears hear, and ~50% watch on mute. Two parts:
- **Title text** — 3–5 words, big, bold, on screen, carrying the context lean. If you
  can say it in a sentence, you can distil it to 3–5 words.
- **The visual itself** — the most compelling image you can show, with *just enough*
  motion. Too much overwhelms; too little bores. A deer stops for movement at the edge
  of its vision — same instinct.

Show while you tell. A talking mouth with dancing captions is the weak version;
complement every claim with something to look at, and point an arrow at the exact
thing you mean.

### Hook mistakes

- **Opaque openers** — "wait till you see this," "you won't believe this." They carry
  no topic. The first line must grab, which means it must be *about* something.
- **Vagueness** — "something crazy happened." No question forms, so no curiosity. Give
  enough to predict on.
- **Burying the value** — for short form the timer is ~4 seconds; for long form ~1–2
  minutes. Deliver a hit of value at or right after the hook. Anything after they
  click off may as well be a black screen.

## The six story locks — phrases that lock attention in

Small, repeatable verbal moves the best creators use on purpose. Contrast (below) is
the engine under all six.

### 1. Term branding

Give your concept a *name*, don't just explain it. The labelling effect: the moment a
thing has a name it feels more important, and an unfamiliar name creates instant
anticipation ("wait, what's that?") that acts like mental quicksand — they can't leave
until it's explained. An electrician who calls their process "the ten-minute safety
sweep" makes it land where "I check a few things" never would; a named framework feels
like a thing you can own, not just advice. Before you publish, ask: can I package this
idea and give it a name?

### 2. Embedded truths

Frame the thing as already true rather than hedged. "**When** you try this," not "**if**
you try this." "If" gives a fork in the road — a moment to evaluate and maybe leave.
"When" removes the fork. Replace *if / maybe / might / could / probably* with *when /
the reason this works / once you do this*. Every hedge is a small exit door.

### 3. Thought narration

Say out loud what the audience is already thinking. *"Now the question you're probably
asking is…"*, *"if I were you, the thing on my mind right now would be…"*. Two payoffs:
they trust you (you clearly know the space), and they keep watching to hear your answer
to their own thought. It turns an internal monologue into a one-on-one conversation at
scale.

### 4. Negative frames

The brain prioritises threats over rewards — loss aversion; people are roughly twice as
motivated to avoid pain as to gain. A warning locks attention faster than the same
information as advice. "Use this hook format" → "stop making your hooks like this."
"Here's how to grow" → "this is killing your growth."

> **Framing flag.** This technique's default surface form is negative and pain-led. It
> is documented in full because framing is the owner's choice
> ([`content-rules.md`](content-rules.md)). A positive-only brand keeps the underlying
> *contrast* (expected vs true) and drops the negative surface: "this is killing your
> growth" → "this one change unlocks your growth." The gap survives; the pain framing
> doesn't. Owners who use pain framing use it as written.

### 5. Loop openers

A phrase dropped in to flip the attention hourglass over and reset the clock — reopen a
second loop as the first closes. Confirm what you just covered was valuable, then tease
that the next thing is even better *for a different reason*. "Here's the thing though,"
"but that's not even the best part," "most people stop here, but…". Cadence: every
20–30 seconds for short form, every 60–90 for long. If a piece has five sections, it
wants three or four loop openers.

### 6. Contrast words

The engine itself. "**But**" is the strongest — it doesn't just transition, it creates a
comparison. Others: *actually* (what you thought was wrong), *instead* (an
alternative), *turns out* (a reveal), *except* (an exception), *yet* (a
contradiction — "everyone knows this, yet almost nobody does it"). Go through every
clean-point sentence and try to split it in half with a contrast word: lean them one
way, turn them the other.

## Rhythm — write music, not monotone

Sentences of the same length in a row become a drone; the ear demands variety. Vary
short, medium, and long. A short punch lands; a long sentence that builds with energy
carries momentum; the mix is what "sings."

**The jagged-edge test.** Write each sentence on its own line. Look straight down the
left margin: if the line-ends form a jagged edge, the lengths vary. If they end at
roughly the same column, the rhythm is flat — break it up.

**Staccato at the front.** In the hook, compress to short sentences. Short forces
clarity and raises value-per-word. Start short, expand to medium and long as the piece
runs.

**Tags.** Little 3–4 word jabs ("it's wild," "no joke," "watch this") dropped between
longer sentences to break up plotting and reset the ear.

## Tone — one close friend, not an audience

The most magnetic creators sound like they're in the room with you. That conversational
tone breaks the "am I being sold to?" barrier — the listener stops evaluating and just
follows. Write and film as if talking to one specific friend: type the script like a
text or a voice note to them. It gets better with reps; mostly it's forgetting the
camera is a camera.

## Direction — start at the end (the last dab)

The best place to start writing is the *ending*. Decide the last line first, then work
backwards and build the dance in the middle.

The **last dab** is the final line, written to be so memorable that if it were the only
thing someone heard, they'd repeat it to a friend. In looping short form it's also the
setup for the replay — the nine-hitter setting the table for the top of the order. A
practical way in: write the first line and the last line, leave a gap, then fill the
middle.

## Story lenses and the virality formula — being a category of one

A topic alone rarely differentiates; dozens of people cover the same thing. A **story
lens** is your unique angle — the prism the plain beam of light passes through. When
Taylor Swift went to the Super Bowl, the common lenses were her outfit and her
reactions; the uncommon lens was the business impact she drove for the NFL. Same event,
category-of-one angle.

The **virality formula** ties the lens to the idea:

- **Uncommon idea + normal lens** — a genuinely weird/rare idea needs no spin; just
  tell it straight.
- **Common idea + unique lens** — a well-worn topic needs your angle to stand out.

Plus two multipliers:
- **Addressable audience size** — how many people would share this if it crossed their
  feed? Stack overlapping audiences (an Iron Man build appeals to Marvel fans *and*
  engineering fans). You want an unnaturally large applicable audience.
- **Validation** — check whether a similar idea already performed somewhere. Proven
  outliers are the safest to remix.

## The modern story arc — and the seven mistakes that flatten it

The traditional arc (long background → rising action → climax → resolution) is built
for a captive audience — a two-hour film, a 300-page book. On a feed, ~80% bounce
before the rising action. The modern arc starts hot and waves:

Open at ~70/100 intensity immediately. Spike to ~90 with conflict or contrast inside
the first minute. Release slowly to ~30 like a wave. Build back to ~75 with another
loop or head fake. Repeat on a 2–5 minute cadence to the end. Three differences from
the old arc: the intro starts *above zero*, the first stakes moment comes *early*, and
there are *many more peaks*.

The seven mistakes that flatten it:

| # | Mistake | The fix |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Traditional arc** — slow build | Start at 70, spike early, wave to the end |
| 2 | **Jumbled W's** — leading with where/when | Order the stack: **what + why** first, then **who + how**, then **when + where**. Lead with the cake, not the icing. |
| 3 | **No re-hooking** — one buy-in, never re-sold | Close a loop, open a new one; tee up fresh stakes after solving the first. Average view duration is what the algorithm rewards. |
| 4 | **No villain** — a hero with no antagonist | Name the thing/person/idea your hero is against ("they do X, we do Y"). Contrast makes it a blockbuster. |
| 5 | **Nothing to root for** — an unlikeable or unrelatable hero | Build common ground so the viewer sees themselves as the protagonist. Optimism wins. |
| 6 | **No atomic sharability** — can't be retold in one line | Condense to the smallest unit someone can repeat after one watch (the "British are coming" effect). Say each point twice: once like to a colleague, once as a metaphor like to a child. |
| 7 | **Not painting the picture** — no visual | Show, don't only tell. The brain is built for visual input; words alone cause comprehension loss. A picture beats a thousand words. |

## Simplification — holds people can climb

Take complex ideas and shrink them to something anyone grasps, paired with a matching
visual. Think of content like climbing a wall: give people holds — simple concepts,
familiar names, relatable references — to pull themselves up with you.

- *"A pneumatic sensor system linking the suit to his movement"* → *"when he flexes,
  it flexes."*
- Rename the abstract to the familiar: an unfamiliar product becomes "the [known
  thing] version of [known category]."

Say the point twice: once plainly, once as a metaphor. Cut every word that isn't
carrying weight.

## Banned framings (craft edition)

- **"And then" as a connector** — replace with "but" / "therefore" or it's a flat list.
- **Opaque hooks** — "wait till you see this" carries no topic; the first line must be
  about something.
- **Vague stakes** — "something crazy happened" forms no question; give enough to
  predict on.
- **A loop you never close** — every open question needs its payoff, or the audience
  leaves unsatisfied and learns not to trust the next open loop.
- **Five-ask endings** — one clear next action; five asks is no ask.
- **Fabricated evidence** — never invent a number, a quote, or a testimonial to make a
  point land. Anchor real claims, or soften to what's honestly true
  ([`content-rules.md`](content-rules.md)).

## Common mistakes (don't re-walk these)

| Mistake | Fix |
|---|---|
| Draft reads as a list joined by "and then" | Force every seam to a "but" or "therefore" |
| Hook is opaque or vague | State the topic and open a specific question in line one |
| Sentences all the same length | Run the jagged-edge test; vary short / medium / long, add tags |
| Sounds like a lecture at an audience | Rewrite as a voice note to one friend |
| Loop opened and forgotten | Pay off every open question; re-hook at the seam where it closes |
| No angle — same as everyone | Pick a story lens; be a category of one |
| Head fake comes from nowhere | Set up the expected prediction first, then turn against exactly that |
| Complex idea stated once, abstractly | Say it twice — plain, then metaphor — and pair a visual |
| Value saved for the end | Front-load a real hit within the first ~4s (short) or ~1–2 min (long) |
| Ending trails off | Write the last dab first: one memorable, repeatable line |
