# Business Method — how this system thinks about any business

This is the BOS's business brain: how it diagnoses what is actually holding a
business back and prescribes the few moves that change the trajectory. Skills
load the section they need; nothing here is customer-facing copy. The method is
synthesized from the published teachings of Alex Hormozi (Acquisition.com) with
complementary doctrine where his material is thin (April Dunford on positioning,
Michael Gerber on systemization, local-services and micro-business research);
the provenance note at the end says what came from where and which numbers are
directional rather than verified.

**How to use this file:** run the diagnosis (§2-§3) before prescribing anything.
Every prescription targets the ONE identified constraint (§4). The domain
sections (§6-§14) are the levers; the prescription table (§16) maps situations
to moves to the BOS skill that executes them. The per-shape adapters (§15)
translate everything for the seven business shapes in
`knowledge/industry-notes.md`.

**The vocabulary is the system's own (hard rule).** The owner never hears a
source's coined term or a guru's name; every concept surfaces in plain
language or under the names in THIS file. Source names and coinages live only
in this file's provenance spots (the header above, §3's label, and the §19
crosswalk) — never in a skill body, a chat reply, or anything the owner reads.
If a reply is about to use a source's brand-name for a framework, use ours.

## §1. The operating stance

Seven beliefs that govern every diagnosis and prescription:

1. **The business is only ever limited by one constraint at a time.** Fixing
   anything else is motion, not progress.
2. **The owner's self-diagnosis is data, not the diagnosis.** Owners propose
   more of what they're already good at; the constraint is usually the
   uncomfortable thing. Respect the owner, verify with numbers.
3. **Value is perceived.** An offer's worth lives in the buyer's head: the
   outcome they picture, how sure they are it will happen, how fast, and how
   little they have to do. All four are improvable without changing fulfilment.
4. **Volume and simplicity beat cleverness.** Most "strategy problems" are
   volume problems wearing a strategy costume. Simple scales; fancy fails.
5. **Cash timing IS strategy for a small business.** The same revenue,
   collected earlier, funds the next customer. Money-in-hand this month beats
   ratio-on-paper.
6. **Retention out-earns acquisition.** A customer kept costs less than a
   customer won, and churn sets a mathematical ceiling on size (§11).
7. **Do more of what works, then make it better, and only then do something
   new.** Risk rises in that order; new resets the learning clock to zero.

The strategy filter for any idea, from anyone, including the owner: *does this
get more customers, or make current customers worth more?* Neither → it is not
the priority, however exciting.

## §2. The intake (run this before prescribing anything)

Get the owner's four-part statement, then walk the numbers ladder. In BOS this
folds into `/start-here` and the owner's profile; for a diagnosis session, ask
for whatever is missing. Plain language with the owner, always; the labels below
are internal.

**The four-part statement:**
1. "I sell [WHAT] to [WHO]" — offer and avatar in one sentence.
2. Current revenue (monthly or annual run rate).
3. The goal ("I'd like to be at $Y by [when]") — used to test vehicle fit: some
   business models cannot reach some goals, and the mismatch is itself a finding.
4. "What's stopping me is [Z]" — the self-diagnosis. Record it. Do not trust it
   yet (§1.2).

**The numbers ladder (in this order; compute out loud in plain words):**
1. Primary lead source, and the split between sources.
2. Monthly acquisition spend (ads, tools, commissions) ÷ new customers per
   month → **CAC**.
3. Price point, purchase frequency, how long a customer stays → **LTGP**
   (lifetime GROSS PROFIT: revenue minus direct delivery cost, per customer,
   over their life). Always gross profit, never revenue — a revenue-based
   ratio prescribes scaling on fake math.
4. Gross margin on delivery.
5. Churn / repeat rate (restate it back inverted to confirm: "so you keep
   about 60% each year?").
6. Leads per month → conversations → sales; close rate (the owner's own vs
   anyone else who sells).
7. **The capacity probe:** "How many more customers could you take right now?"
   → "What breaks first if you doubled?" → and if nothing breaks: "then what's
   stopped you doubling?" (that answer usually names the real constraint).

**Stop rules:**
- The moment ONE number is clearly out of line (churn way above normal for a
  sticky service; hundreds of content pieces producing a handful of leads; the
  owner closing at 80% while their hire closes at 30%), stop drilling. That
  number is the constraint. Diagnose against it only.
- If the numbers contradict the self-diagnosis, say so plainly and kindly, with
  the arithmetic shown ("your close rate is strong — winning more calls isn't
  the thing to fix; look at how few calls there are").
- Missing numbers are a finding, not a blocker: "we don't know your churn" makes
  *measure it* the first prescription (a scoreboard beats a guess, §12).

**How `/start-here` runs this intake (the first-run consultation).**
[FOUNDER-RULED 2026-07-03, revises the 2026-07-02 "useful-now threshold → put it
to work" framing: the Day-1 win is the consultation itself, not an artifact; see
`docs/architecture/2026-07-03-collaborative-consultation-design.md`.] The intake
above is not a form to fire off at once, nor a budget to spend and stop. In
`/start-here` it *is* the Day-1 win: after a zero-question reflection of
understanding (the "how did it know" taste), the aim of the conversation is that
the owner feels a sharp operator thinking *with* them about their business. The
system gets sharper the more it is told, and the owner should feel that.

- **The hinge (get it before prescribing anything bigger than the taste).** Draw
  out the goal (§2 item 3) and the owner's own theory of the blocker (§2 item 4)
  as the required anchor. Everything after fills the blanks *that goal* needs. The
  stated blocker is data, not the diagnosis (§1.2) — earn the real one by
  reasoning it through with them, never route straight off the relief word.
- **Read engagement live (the gauge), and let it choose the whole shape.** Rich,
  expansive answers → the consultation IS the win: run the loop in service of the
  goal, each question built on the last, showing your working (the business-brain
  logic that proves you grasp the nuances), as deep as engagement sustains.
  Clipped one-liners / low-patience ("just tell me what this does") → do NOT
  consult; hand them a fast tangible keyless win aimed at what they named, and
  deepen later on their terms. Ambiguous → open the consultation lightly; if they
  lean in, deepen; if they stay clipped, give the quick win (the churn-safe
  tie-break).
- **The stop rule is scoped to a focused diagnosis session, not this
  consultation.** In a diagnosis you stop the moment one number is clearly out of
  line (that IS the constraint). In the first-run consultation the aim is a felt
  understanding of the whole picture in service of the goal, so keep co-exploring
  the economics the goal needs (what the work makes, how full the week is, how
  many they keep) rather than quitting at the first signal. Still 1-3 moves out,
  never eight pages (§4.7).
- **The build comes after, and as a recommendation.** Once the understanding has
  earned it, reflect what you now grasp (the goal, the real constraint) and lead
  with the one build you would recommend, plus a couple of alternatives — never a
  cold "pick one of three." The recommendation always follows the give, never
  stacks on a question.
- **Backstop, not a target.** The gauge is the real control. As a backstop only,
  if an engaged owner passes roughly 8-10 exchanges and the real constraint still
  will not name itself, reflect where you have got to and move to a first build —
  it will reveal more than more questions would.
- **Deepening is continuous.** What the owner declines now stays available every
  session (the profile marker tracks how far the intake got, `intake_depth=`);
  diagnosis is a loop (§4.3), so the intake resumes and compounds rather than
  being a one-shot gate.

## §3. The constraint rubric

> This six-item rubric is the BOS's operationalization of Hormozi's practice
> (his recurring frame is "get more customers, make them worth more, keep them
> longer" plus the capacity probe); it is not his verbatim list. Labelled per
> the anti-drift doctrine.

Exactly one of these is the binding constraint. The signals tell you which; the
numbers ladder provides them.

| # | Constraint | Signals it's this one |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **LEADS (demand)** | Few enquiries relative to effort; idle capacity; owner takes whatever arrives rather than choosing. Advice/professional shapes are most often demand-constrained. |
| 2 | **SALES / CONVERSION** | Leads exist but few become customers; close rate low, or a big founder-vs-team close gap (motion problem, not market problem); enquiries answered slowly; quotes/proposals die in "sent". |
| 3 | **DELIVERY / CAPACITY** | Calendar/trucks/chairs full; enquiries turned away; what breaks on doubling is fulfilment or hiring. At under ~$10k/mo this is usually fake — recheck #6. |
| 4 | **RETENTION / CHURN** | Growth stalls despite healthy CAC; the ceiling equation (§11.1) caps size near current size; one-off customers who never return. |
| 5 | **PROFIT-PER-UNIT** | Busy and full but no money left; margin below the bands in §8.1; underpricing (close rate suspiciously high, §8.2); comp or costs eating the margin. |
| 6 | **OWNER (focus or hours)** | Two businesses/avatars splitting attention; unmade decisions blocking everything; or the owner IS delivery and the calendar is full (capacity-flavour). "Nothing would break if I doubled" → this row. |

**Order of examination when several look plausible:** retention → profit-per-unit
→ sales/conversion → leads. (A leads prescription into a leaky bucket or a
loss-making unit wastes money; check the bucket before the tap.) Delivery and
owner announce themselves through the capacity probe.

**The decision-impasse check (the seven stuck-points).** When the constraint
seems to be an unmade decision rather than a number, look for these classic
impasses — each is a choice between hard-today and hard-forever:

1. Serving 2-3 avatars instead of one.
2. A decision stalled "until we have data".
3. A second business splitting focus (one $2M business beats two $1M
   businesses).
4. Expansion before the talent exists.
5. A comp structure that underpays (can't hire) or overpays (no margin).
6. Underpricing.
7. A single product with no next offer (the customer base is a pool of
   qualified buyers for a back end that doesn't exist yet).

**The data-stall rule (applies to impasse 2):** if you could decide, fail, and
reverse in LESS time than gathering the data would take, deciding now is
correct — waiting is the failure in judgment.

## §4. One-constraint execution

1. **All deliberate effort on the one constraint.** Sequential beats parallel;
   the other fires create urgency for this fix. "Could we improve that other
   thing? Probably. Is it the constraint? No."
2. **The standing engine:** the priority underneath the priority — keep
   selling, keep delivering. The proven engine never stops while the constraint
   is attacked.
3. **Expect bottleneck ping-pong.** Fix conversion → volume swamps delivery →
   delivery fix shifts it back to leads. That is the system working. Re-run the
   ladder (§2) after each fix lands; diagnosis is a loop.
4. **More → Better → New, in strict order.**
   - MORE: 10x the volume of what already works. Ask "what actually stops us
     doing 10x?" (usually: hours → a delegation cue, §12).
   - BETTER: when volume is maxed, fix the worst funnel stage (§10.3), then go
     back to MORE. Boundary rule: changing creative, hooks, the lead magnet,
     the offer's name or wrapper on an EXISTING channel is Better. A new
     platform or a new channel cell is New. (This is why "swap the magnet"
     precedes "try TikTok".)
   - NEW: only when More and Better are exhausted. Highest risk; the default
     owner instinct; usually wrong.
   - Budget form: ~70% on proven winners, ~20% variations, ~10% genuinely new
     (directional).
5. **Change discipline:** test any change in a contained slice (one segment,
   10% of the list) before rolling out; expect a short-term dip after any
   change (directional: ~20%) and don't panic-revert.
6. **Speed is king.** When the fix is known, the question is "what are you
   doing in the next two hours that matters more?" Same-day execution loops
   compound enormously over a year.
7. **The output of a diagnosis is 1-3 moves.** Eight pages of notes executed at
   once is a failure mode, not thoroughness.

## §5. Stage-based sequencing

Prescriptions change with size. Most BOS owners sit in the first two rows; the
later rows exist so the system knows what NOT to prescribe early.

| Stage | Focus | Deliberately ignore |
|---|---|---|
| **~$0-10k/mo** | Cash over perfection: sell before building, collect up front. Warm outreach to existing contacts first, then ONE channel. Owner does all selling. Take any reasonable customer (aim deliberate acquisition at one avatar, but take the money). | Logo/website polish, hiring, second offers, automation for its own sake, "passive income" structures |
| **~$10-50k/mo** | The Rule of One: one avatar, one product, one channel until ~$1M/yr. Raise prices (his standing workshop claim, directional: about half the room is underpriced). Fix the sales motion. Document what you do as you do it; first hires take the owner's lowest-value hours. | New avatars/products/channels, personal brand as a project, a second location |
| **~$1-5M/yr** | Retention and LTV become the multiplier. Profit is a discipline (set the line, keep it). One big bet a year on top of the standing engine. Reinvest in the two capital items of a service business: talent and reputation. Weekly testing cadence. | Founder bootstrapping every new function personally; parallel constraint-fixing; diversifying into a second business |
| **~$5M+/yr** | Buy knowledge: hire people who have already built the function. The owner allocates like an investor. Talent attraction becomes a second funnel. | The founder as doer; untested company-wide changes |

Skill-stacking order for the OWNER as they grow: something to sell → promotion
→ persuasion → leadership/operations. Prescribe learning in that order too.

## §6. The value equation (score any offer before touching price or leads)

**Value = (the Arrival × the Belief) ÷ (the Wait × the Work).** Four
variables, all *perceived* — the buyer's head, not the spec sheet. The
denominator is the higher-leverage side: most small operators compete on
bigger promises; the money is in faster and easier.

**Scoring procedure (0-5 each; run on the owner's actual offer):**
1. **The Arrival** (what they end up with) — does the offer name the
   destination the buyer pictures, or the deliverable? 0 = "6 sessions of
   physio", 5 = "back on the netball court by March".
2. **The Belief** (how sure they are it will work for THEM) — what proof,
   specificity, mechanism, and guarantee back the promise? 0 = bare claim,
   5 = named guarantee + local proof + visible mechanism.
3. **The Wait** — how soon does the buyer FEEL a first win? 0 = months and
   invisible, 5 = an engineered win within days of paying.
4. **The Work** — what must the buyer still do, learn, or give up?
   0 = heavy DIY, 5 = done-for-you.

**Stop rules:** the lowest-scoring variable is the offer's constraint — fix it
before the others. Do not raise price while the Belief scores 0-2 (premium
prices on low-certainty offers buy refunds and resentment). "Fast beats free":
when in doubt, cut the denominator.

**Raising each variable:** Arrival — sharpen, don't inflate; state it in the
buyer's own words and status terms. Belief — proof, guarantee (§7.2),
narrower avatar, visible mechanism. Wait — engineer a first win in the first
days (this is also the retention lever, §11.3). Work — move steps from
they-do to you-do; remove decisions and homework.

## §7. Offer construction

**7.0 The market gate (run first).** Offer work is wasted on a dead market.
Four pass/fail checks on the target avatar:

1. **Real pain** — a need, not a mild want.
2. **Purchasing power** — they can pay the price that makes the business work.
3. **Findability** — a channel or watering hole exists to reach them.
4. **Not shrinking** — the market is stable or growing.

**Stop rule:** a hard fail on pain or purchasing power → reposition the avatar
before writing an offer word. Most "offer problems" are avatar problems.

**Niche rule (every BOS-sized business): NARROWER.** Under ~$10M/yr,
specificity raises price and believability simultaneously (directional
illustration: the same course, re-aimed per audience rung, sells at ~100x
between "generic" and "hyper-specific").

**Commit rule:** one flopped offer is not market feedback; iterate offers
WITHIN the niche rather than switching niches (switching resets all learning
to zero).

**7.1 The Category-of-One build (5 steps).**
1. Write the dream outcome as an arrival scene in the buyer's words.
2. List EVERY problem between the buyer and it — before, during, after
   purchase; through four lenses per step (won't work for me / too slow / too
   hard / not worth it). A short list means you stopped early.
3. Flip each problem into a solution statement ("how to [reverse of problem]").
   This is the checklist the offer must cover.
4. Brainstorm delivery vehicles per solution. Two forcing questions: "if they
   paid 10x, what would we deliver?" (reveals the premium tier) and "if they
   paid a tenth, how would we still get them the result?" (reveals the scalable
   assets).
5. Trim & stack: cut high-cost/low-value items; keep high-value/low-cost
   (templates, checklists, recordings — expensive once, free forever); present
   as a NAMED bundle whose summed value dwarfs the price.
   **Completeness rule:** the bundle must answer every listed problem; the one
   unsolved problem becomes the reason they don't buy.
   **Bundle test:** solves all problems, genuinely one of a kind, impossible to
   price-compare with the shop down the street. Fail any → rework.

**7.2 Guarantee picker (risk reversal).** Structure: "If you don't get X by Y,
we Z" — without the Z it's decoration. Types and fit:
- **Unconditional** (money back, no questions): low-ticket, consumer, low
  marginal cost. Strongest converter.
- **Conditional** ("do these steps; if no result, we [refund / keep working
  free / better-than-money-back]"): high-ticket and B2B; make the conditions
  the exact actions a successful customer takes anyway.
- **Anti-guarantee** ("all sales final, because you see everything up front"):
  IP-exposing or bingeable offers. Needs the stated reason.
- **Implied / performance** (paid on results): measurable-outcome services;
  the strongest possible likelihood signal.
Design procedure: write down the buyer's biggest fear about paying, invert that
exact fear, name the guarantee vividly. Decision rules: high fulfilment cost →
conditional/anti; low ticket → unconditional; business buyer → specific
conditional. **Compliance overrides:** consumer law mandates remedies
regardless of stated guarantees (in AU, anti-guarantees can be unlawful for
consumer purchases), and regulated shapes (§15: clinic, finance) must never
guarantee outcomes — use service-level guarantees instead (response time,
turn-up-on-time, we-handle-everything).

**7.3 Bonuses.** A core offer plus NAMED bonuses out-persuades the same content
as one lump. Rules: every bonus has a name with a benefit in it, a stated
value, proof, and kills one specific "reason I won't succeed"; tools and
templates beat more training; total stated bonus value should eclipse the
price. In 1:1 sales, ask for the sale first — bonuses reveal after the yes, or
answer the specific objection after a no ("I can't move the price, but I can
add [the bonus matching their concern] — fair enough?").

**7.4 Scarcity and urgency (both must be TRUE).** Scarcity = quantity:
servicing caps, weekly intake caps, cohort caps, limited drops; set the cap
below expected demand, sell out, then say so. Urgency = time: rolling cohorts
("today's yes starts Monday"), seasonal windows with real end dates,
bonus/price deadlines, and the one sanctioned discount-shaped move — a real
price RISE announced with a deadline. Fake caps and fake countdowns destroy the
Belief variable and, for some shapes, are traps outright (§15).

**7.5 Naming (the five parts) and the refresh order.** Name offers with 3-5
of: a reason-why hook, who it's for, the result, the timeframe, and a container
word (Challenge, Blueprint, Sprint, System). The result is stated in the owner's
own framing (§18). When response
decays, refresh in cost order: creative → body copy → headline/name → duration
→ enhancer → structure. The same offer renamed is new to the market; renaming
is a near-free lead-flow reset.

**7.6 The commodity test.** Put the owner's offer next to their nearest
competitor's with logos stripped: if a stranger can't tell them apart in 10
seconds, it's a commodity — run 7.1. Escaping commodity pricing compounds
across response rate × close rate × price simultaneously.

## §8. Pricing doctrine

**8.1 The margin gauge (service shapes).** Three bands: **below ~50% gross
margin = emergency** — redesign delivery now (§11.6) before any growth move;
**50-80% = below target** — plan margin work; **~80%+ = healthy** for service
businesses. (Product/ecommerce shapes judge margin against their own cash
cycle instead, §15.)

**8.2 The close-rate signal (directional table).** Close rate on qualified
sales conversations is a free, live price signal: 80%+ ≈ underpriced 3-4x;
60-80% ≈ underpriced 2-3x; 50-60% ≈ 1.5-2x; 40-50% ≈ 1.25-1.5x; ~35-40% =
about right; **under ~30% → do NOT cut price** — fix the sales motion or the
avatar first.
**8.3 The capacity rule (micro-business inversion).** When the owner is at or
near delivery capacity with steady enquiries, the correct move is PRICE, not
volume. Raising prices either lifts profit directly or sheds the
least-profitable work and recovers capacity; price becomes the filter. This
overrides any volume prescription while it holds.

**8.4 How to raise prices (procedure).** (1) Build the value story first (§7)
so the rise is earned; (2) raise for NEW customers first; (3) announce to the
pipeline with a real deadline ("locks in the current rate"); (4) reinvest
visible margin into delivery — that reinvestment is what makes the price
virtuous rather than merely higher. The virtuous cycle: higher price → more
committed customers → better results → more proof → better customers → margin
funds better delivery → more value. Cheap prices spin the same wheel backward.

**8.5 The discount rule.** *You may design a cheaper offer; you may never
cheapen the offer on the table.* Reactive same-scope price cuts mid-negotiation
teach buyers that prices are fiction. Engineered structures are fine and are
not discounts: attraction offers (§9.3), prepay rewards, downsells that change
scope or payment shape (§9.4), continuity joining benefits, and barter (a price
concession traded for reviews, referrals, or testimonials). Never price off
"competitor average minus 10%": the competitors being copied are broke.

**8.6 Price floors.** Compute the true cost floor (materials, loaded labour,
overhead share, callback/refund risk) before any quote; never price below it
knowingly. New, unproven offers may launch cheap to harvest early yeses — then
ratchet upward using 8.2 until the yes-rate lands in the healthy band. Premium
doctrine (8.4) governs once the offer is proven.

## §9. The money model (offer sequencing and cash velocity)

**9.1 Definition and build order.** A money model is the SEQUENCE of offers —
what, when, how — engineered so a new customer's early cash funds the next
customer. Build one stage at a time, in order, each until reliable:
1. **Get cash:** one attraction offer that reliably turns strangers into
   customers.
2. **Get more cash:** add ONE upsell; then a downsell to rescue the nos.
3. **Get the most cash:** add continuity (recurring revenue), sold last in the
   early window.
One offer at a time; measure in quarters. Warning that is also a forecast:
when the money model starts working, operations become the next constraint
(§4.3).

**9.2 The 30-day cash test (two tiers).**
- **Survival bar:** cash collected from a new customer in their first 30 days
  covers CAC + cost of fulfilment. Below this, marketing spend is a loan the
  business makes to itself.
- **Self-funding bar:** 30-day gross profit >
  2 × (CAC + fulfilment cost) — one customer funds the acquisition and
  servicing of the next two. The 30-day window matters because it matches
  standard interest-free payment cycles: pass it and acquisition can float on
  costless credit indefinitely.
Report which tier the business is at; prescribe toward the next one. (10x is
the aspiration, not the bar.)

**9.3 Attraction offer menu (pick ONE, match to shape §15):** win-your-money-
back (pay up front, hit defined actions/results, earn it back as store credit
toward the next purchase — expect a minority to claim it, directional ~10%);
qualified giveaways (raffle the premium service so every entrant is an ideal
buyer; offer runners-up a starter version); decoy (advertise the free/DIY
version, present the premium beside it); buy-X-get-Y; pay-less-now vs
pay-more-later; free-with-consumption. Free/discounted/paid are points on one
risk-reduction continuum; profit comes from the behaviour the offer triggers.

**9.4 Upsells and downsells.**
- **Upsell timing:** every solved problem reveals the next one; sell the next
  solution at the moment the next problem appears (internal timing logic —
  the customer-facing line names the next win, §18). Five moments: at
  purchase, at activation/first win, midway, at a milestone, before expiry.
- **Upsell mechanics:** the classic pair ("you can't have X without Y" — offer
  the highest-margin add-on first); the A/B ask ("almond croissant or banana
  bread with that?" — never "anything else?"); anchor (show the premium
  option first); rollover (credit past spend toward the bigger commitment);
  named bundles (one ask, many items); tier the menu so the middle option is
  the intended sale.
- **Downsell rules:** a downsell is any offer made after a no. NEVER the same
  thing cheaper (that's a discount, 8.5). Change what they get or how they
  pay: prepay reward → third-party finance/card → half now half on payday →
  even instalments → (services) a feature-trimmed tier with the removed items
  named and valued. Re-confirm desire partway ("out of ten, how much do you
  still want this?" — proceed at 8+). Bill on paydays to cut declines.
- **Continuity:** attach only where ongoing value genuinely exists.
  Join-today mechanics: a bonus worth more than the first payment (advertise
  the bonus, not the membership), first-period benefits, rate-lock, or a
  setup fee waived for commitment. Quarterly/annual billing cuts churn versus
  monthly. Immediately after the first continuity yes, offer the prepaid
  multi-period option (more up-front cash, 9.2).

**9.5 Cash pull-forward toolkit.** Reward prepayment (never "interest" framing
— same math, present prepay as the saving); deposits and setup fees; bill on
completion day, not net-30; align charge dates with customer paydays;
third-party financing (you're paid today); move monthly to quarterly/annual.
For a small operator, resequencing the SAME revenue earlier is often the
single fastest "growth" move available.

**9.6 Gap-filling with partners.** Missing an offer stage? Sell someone else's
product at commission (the physio's rehab equipment, the educator's software
referrals) before building anything.

## §10. Acquisition

**10.1 The four doors.** All advertising is one of four cells: warm 1-to-1
(warm outreach), warm 1-to-many (content), cold 1-to-1 (cold outreach), cold
1-to-many (paid ads). Adoption order for a small business:
1. **Warm outreach until ~10 paying customers exist.** Free, near-certain,
   always first. Pattern per message: acknowledge something specific about
   them, a genuine compliment, one small clear ask — and prefer "who do you
   know who…?" over pitching the contact directly.
2. **Content next** (compounds, warms every later channel).
3. **Then ONE of** cold outreach (time-rich) or paid ads (cash-rich).
4. **Master one channel before adding another.** Mastered = predictable
   cost-per-lead and conversion, a documented playbook a hire could run, and
   10x volume wouldn't break it.

**10.2 Volume doctrine, gated.** GATE FIRST: *is the owner at delivery
capacity?* At capacity → the capacity rule (8.3) overrides volume: price up,
route volume through systems (automated follow-up, review asks, reactivation —
the BOS itself is the mechanism: the system does the 100, the owner does 5).
Not at capacity + leads constraint → the **volume floor**: 100 primary actions
a day (100 warm/cold reach-outs, 100 minutes of content, or ~$100 of ad work)
for 100 straight days on the one channel before judging it. A channel judged
on a fraction of that volume was never tested. Graduate to open-to-goal ("5
booked calls today, however long it takes") once the channel is dialled.

**10.3 Engaged-lead math (the "I need more leads" rebuttal).** Count 30 days
of: attempts/reach → engaged leads (showed interest) → conversations → sales.
Compute the three conversion rates; fix the WORST stage, not the top. Weak
engagement from decent volume = magnet/offer problem (10.4). Engaged leads but
few conversations = speed and cadence (respond inside 5 minutes in business
hours — evidence-backed; 60-second automation is the aspiration; follow up
persistently, directional 5+ touches). Conversations but few sales = sales
motion (§12.5) or offer (§7). Directional: engaged-lead-to-customer under ~5%
is an offer problem, and a lead unworked for ~3 months has gone cold — a
worked 200-name list beats a hoarded 5,000.

**10.4 Lead magnets.** A complete free solution to one NARROW problem that
sits one step before the core offer, good enough that people would pay for it
("give away the secrets, sell the implementation"). Three types: reveal-a-
problem (a diagnostic — best when the problem worsens with delay; frame the
name as the gain: "get your borrowing-power score", per §18), sample/trial
(best for recurring needs), one-step-of-many (best for complex jobs). Name it
like an offer (7.5); make the next step explicit. The magnet swap is the
cheapest "more leads" fix that actually works (it's a Better move, §4.4).

**10.5 The local gravity stack (overrides four-doors ordering for locally
bought shapes — trades, clinics, hospitality, many services).** Strict
priority; don't advance a tier until the one above holds:
1. **Answer speed** (missed-call text-back, 5-minute responses — first
   responder wins a large share of local jobs regardless of price).
2. **Review engine** — velocity beats stock: a steady drip of recent reviews,
   asked at the moment of demonstrated satisfaction, by the person who did the
   work, with a direct link; reply to every review (the reply is read by the
   NEXT customer).
3. **Profile completeness** (categories, photos, hours, consistent details).
4. **Proof publishing** (before/afters, job stories mapped to real searches).
5. **Community presence** (partners, adjacent trades, local groups).
6. **Paid local** — last, because ads amplify whatever tiers 1-4 are.
**Hard gate:** no lead-generation prescription for a local business until (a)
enquiries are answered inside 5 minutes in business hours and (b) a review ask
fires on every completed job. Both are cheap; both usually ARE the constraint.

**10.6 Paid ads (when reached).** Anatomy: call out the person (who it's for,
§18) → make the value vivid → one CTA with a real reason to act now. Three phases: track the money first (know cost/lead,
cost/customer, LTGP before spending), budget the losing phase (expect to spend
~1-2x one customer's 30-day profit per test before judging; zero leads at ~1x
→ kill), then scale in 10-20% steps. **Scaling gate:** LTGP:CAC at 3:1 is the
never-scale-below floor; because almost every BOS business has two human
touchpoints (someone sells AND someone delivers), aim ~6:1+ before aggressive
spend. If CAC is normal for the market but the ratio fails, the fix is the
model (price, upsells, retention), not the ads.

**10.7 The referral engine.** Forcing thought: if all future customers had to
come from your current ones, what would you change? Two levers: results worth
talking about, and an engineered ask. Ask at high-emotion moments (right after
a win, at purchase, at job completion); ask for an INTRODUCTION (a three-way
text), not "any names?"; make the reward mutual, one-click, tracked, fast-paid.
Directional benchmark: a healthy small business sees ~25%+ of new customers
from referrals — below that with happy customers means the ask is missing, and
installing it comes before any cold spend.

**10.8 Lead getters (leverage).** Once the owner has mastered one channel,
multiply it through: customers (10.7), an employee trained via the 3Ds
(§12.1) into the PROVEN channel (never delegate an unmastered one), partners/
affiliates (the plumber↔sparky↔inspector pact; supply materials, pay fast,
track cleanly), or an agency on a learn-then-internalize deal (pay extra for
them to explain decisions, take it in-house when your results match theirs).

**10.9 The leads roadmap (levels).** L1: your contacts know what you sell.
L2: one channel at consistent personal max (the volume floor). L3: someone else
runs that channel. L4: the product earns ~25%+ word-of-mouth (directional, 10.7). L5: multiple
team-run channels. Most 2-10 person businesses should live at L3-4; never
prescribe L5 machinery to an L1 owner.

## §11. Retention and delivery

**11.1 The ceiling equation (run this early, it reframes everything).**
Max customers = new customers per month ÷ monthly churn rate. 30 new/month at
10% churn caps the business at 300, forever, regardless of marketing. Halving
churn doubles the ceiling exactly as doubling sign-ups would, and costs less.
**Hard gate:** churn above ~5%/month on a recurring model freezes acquisition-
spend increases until retention is fixed — marketing into a leaky bucket is
net-zero. (Directional: one documented case cut churn 10%→3% and multiplied
LTV ~3.3x.)

**11.2 The four LTV levers, in test-cost order:** price (8.3-8.4) → margin
(11.6) → frequency (continuity AND back-end upsells are different things;
most owners build neither — campaigns to existing customers run at ~90% margin
(directional)
and cost nothing to reach) → duration (11.3-11.5). Always keep a premium tier:
directionally ~20% of customers will pay 3-5x for more access/certainty, and
averaging everyone into the middle leaves it unclaimed.

**11.3 Activation (onboarding-to-first-win).** Almost all churn is decided
early; people quit things they never started. Procedure: define the activation
moment (the earliest event after which customers demonstrably stay); engineer
a felt win inside the first 7 days (14 max) even if small; personally re-sell
the decision inside 48 hours (welcome call/video/note — buyer's remorse peaks
immediately); track "% activated within X days" weekly — it is next quarter's
churn, visible now. Onboarding legitimately costs more than steady-state
delivery; price for it.

**11.4 The retention cadence (generalized from his gym doctrine).** Five
systems, each with a named owner and a weekly checkbox (they decay otherwise):
scheduled personal contact on a fixed cadence (directional: every ~14 days,
genuinely specific to the customer, never a newsletter blast); an
engagement floor checked mid-period with same-week intervention (the customer
who stopped using hasn't consciously quit yet — reach them before they do);
small personal moments at purchase and milestones; engineered community/status
moments (people don't quit places where they have standing); and exit
conversations (11.5). The deeper reframe: the product is the vehicle; the
relationship and accountability are why they stay.

**11.5 The cancellation save.** No silent cancellations: every cancel triggers
a real conversation within 24 hours, run as a discovery call (why did you
join, what changed, what result do you still want), offering a matched repair:
pause instead of cancel, a lighter tier, a restarted onboarding, a fixed
failure. Directional: about half of cancellations are saveable. Log every exit
in three buckets — never activated / product failed them / life happened — the
distribution tells you whether to fix onboarding, delivery, or nothing. Chart
WHEN customers leave; install an intervention one step before the modal exit
point.

**11.6 The delivery grid (margin and owner-hours redesign).** When margin is
below the bands (8.1) or the owner's calendar is the constraint: list every
promise in the offer; for each, walk the dimensions (1:1 vs group vs
one-to-many; done-for-you vs done-with-you vs DIY; live vs recorded vs
template); run the 10x/one-tenth test (§7.1.4); recompose so perceived value
stays high and marginal cost drops — one-to-many for teaching, templates for
repeat questions, 1:1 reserved for the moments that actually move the
customer's outcome. Guard rail: never cut the element customers credit for
their result; cut what is expensive to deliver but invisible in the outcome.
Productize: fixed scope, fixed price, documented process, deliverable-defined.

## §12. Team, owner, and operating cadence

**12.1 The 3Ds delegation loop.** Document (write the checklist YOURSELF while
doing the task — you can't document what you can't do) → Demonstrate (do it in
front of them, narrating) → Duplicate (they do it in front of you until output
matches, graded against a rubric, feedback on the single lowest-scoring item
per round). Expect a documented process to get a competent person to ~80% of
your outcome; the last 20% is judgment — don't script it. Every time reality
beats the checklist, update the checklist.

**12.2 Who-not-how and paying for A-players.** Once a function is proven,
anything NEW starts with "who", not "how" — the business's ceiling is the sum
of the knowledge on the team. Better to pay top-of-market for one A-player
than to underpay for three B-players (A-players attract A-players); hire
before the breaking point; pay on time without exception. People learn two
ways: modelling (watch the best) and role-play (do it with feedback); build
all training on those two.

**12.3 The technician trap (why prescriptions don't stick).** Most owners are
technicians who had an entrepreneurial seizure: they assume that understanding
the WORK means understanding a BUSINESS that does that work — different jobs.
Diagnose the owner's mode (technician does the work, manager orders it,
entrepreneur designs the machine) before prescribing, because a technician
will retreat from any system back into comfortable hands-on work. The question
flip that reframes everything: not "what work has to be done?" but "how must
the business work?" Standing prescription: a recurring, defended block working
ON the business.

**12.4 The prototype build (systemization for a 3-person shop).** Draw the org
chart of the mature business, even at 2 people, with the owner's name in every
box. The business builds itself by replacing the owner's name one box at a
time, bottom-up (technician boxes first). For each box handed over: write the
role's result and standards, then run the 3Ds. A documented business is a
sellable, delegable, survivable asset; an undocumented one is a job with
overhead.

**12.5 The discovery arc (the sales conversation, summary depth).** Six
beats, for founder-led discovery calls, quote visits, and consults:
1. **Hear it** — why they're here, in their words, until a real problem
   surfaces; never pitch to a guess.
2. **Name it** — play it back and get agreement ("so the real issue is X and
   the goal is Y — fair?").
3. **Map what they've tried** — empathy plus positioning as a different path,
   not "the same but better".
4. **Sell the arrival** — the destination they named, tied to the top three
   elements of the solution; never the itinerary of features.
5. **Settle the concerns** — obstacles (logistics, decision-maker, real
   information gaps) get dismantled BEFORE the ask, during beats 1-3;
   objections (after the ask) usually wear one of three costumes:
   circumstances (money/timing), other people (the absent decision-maker —
   get them on the call or ask "if they said yes, would you?"), or self
   (fear — "decisions take information, not time; what's the actual
   concern?"). Loop gently: restate the concern, one relevant story, ask
   again; a NEW concern each loop is progress, the same concern recycling is
   the real one.
6. **Seal it and welcome** — immediately after the yes, a same-day personal
   welcome; buyer's remorse starts at the receipt (hands off to activation,
   11.3).
Ethics line: sell like a coach — help them decide, never corner. Wrong fit →
say so and decline. Clinic shapes: the discovery arc structures the
conversation about logistics and care plans, NEVER the clinical
recommendation (§15).

**12.6 The operating cadence.** One-page weekly scoreboard (leads,
conversations, close rate, cash collected, activation %, churn/saves, plus THE
constraint metric of the quarter — the gap against benchmark picks the week's
priority). Three rhythms, sized down for small teams: a sub-10-minute daily
huddle (yesterday's numbers, a customer win), a scripted weekly review, and a
weekly 1:1 per person where the owner mostly listens. Cadence is what makes
every other section actually happen; a business with no numbers rhythm gets
the scoreboard as its FIRST prescription.

**12.7 When the constraint is the owner (the terminal node).** When the
capacity probe ends at "nothing would break — I just haven't", diagnose which
pattern and prescribe against it. Seven principles:
1. **Volume negates luck.** Nobody is one funnel/tool/hack away; set INPUT
   goals (the reps), not outcome goals, and judge nothing before the volume
   has been run (10.2).
2. **The work works on you.** The reps forge the person who can run the bigger
   business; confidence is built from evidence, so keep the proof visible
   (counts of calls made, posts shipped, quotes sent).
3. **The shiny-object rule.** New opportunities are the enemy, and they get more
   attractive as the business succeeds. Focus is measured by what the owner
   declines. Re-price the shiny object out loud: the new venture costs the
   current one its compounding.
4. **The switching tax.** Every pivot resets compounding to zero. One $2M
   business beats two $1M businesses; the competitors winning are focused on
   one thing.
5. **Boredom is the price.** Boredom is the toll of a working strategy, not a
   signal to change it. When bored: add volume or raise standards inside the
   current game; never change games to escape it.
6. **The conviction horizon.** Judge progress in years, act in days;
   pre-decide that a long stretch of invisible compounding is expected, not
   evidence of failure.
7. **Fear is a mile wide and an inch deep.** The scary move (raise prices,
   make the hire, kill the second business) is almost always smaller in
   reality than in prospect: run the actual arithmetic of the feared outcome,
   take the action once, let the evidence dissolve the fear.

**Diagnostic tells → pattern:** "thinking of also starting…" → shiny-object rule.
"Tried it for a month, didn't work" → volume/horizon failure. "I'll raise
prices once…" → fear dressed as sequencing. "Just need the right funnel/tool"
→ lottery-ticket thinking. "Bit bored with the business" → boredom-as-signal
error. "Nobody does it like me, can't hire" → technician trap + inch-deep
fear. "Going broader, this niche feels tapped" → switching tax incoming.

**Delivery tone (this is how the BOS says hard things):** numbers before
judgment — compute the gap and let the arithmetic deliver the verdict; one
constraint, one move, explicit permission to ignore the rest; frame the
pattern as one every owner falls into, never as a character flaw; attack the
allocation, respect the operator ("you're clearly good — the switching is
what's costing you"); re-price rather than refuse ("that idea costs your
current business its compounding year"); and say it plainly once — hedging
signals you don't believe it either.

## §13. When NOT to grow (the stop-rule checklist)

Sometimes the correct diagnosis output is "the move is not growth." Prescribe
consolidation when ANY of these holds:

1. **Churn above ~5%/month on a recurring model** → freeze acquisition-spend
   increases; fix retention (11.1).
2. **LTGP:CAC below the gate** (3:1 floor; ~6:1 target for two-touchpoint
   businesses) → fix the model (price, upsells, retention), not the traffic
   (10.6).
3. **30-day cash below the survival bar** (9.2) → every new customer deepens
   the hole; fix the money model first.
4. **Delivery margin under ~50%** → growth multiplies a loss; run the delivery
   cube (11.6).
5. **The owner at delivery capacity** → the capacity rule (8.3): price, not
   volume.
6. **The money model just started working** → expect operations to become the
   constraint; strengthen delivery before pouring fuel (9.1).
7. **Quality varies wildly by who delivers** → document (12.1/12.4) before
   amplifying inconsistency.
8. **Profit is a discipline:** if there is never money left, set the profit
   line first and make the business fit it, not the reverse.

## §14. Positioning (the ladder that precedes the offer)

Run this when the owner can't say why customers should pick them, and before
any brand-strategy work. Strict order — starting anywhere else produces
neat-but-undifferentiated positioning:

1. **List real competitive alternatives** — what customers would ACTUALLY do
   instead, including "do nothing", "DIY/spreadsheet", "the brother-in-law",
   and "put it off a year". Exclude phantom competitors that never appear in
   real deals.
2. **Isolate unique, provable attributes** — capabilities or facts the
   alternatives can't truthfully claim (speed, guarantee, credential,
   locality, process).
3. **Translate attributes to value** — ask "so what?" twice per attribute
   until it lands on a customer outcome; cluster into 1-3 themes.
4. **Find who cares most** — the segment for whom those themes matter
   disproportionately. The niche is DERIVED here, not picked by taste (this is
   what makes the §7.0 niche rule rigorous).
5. **Pick an existing market category** that makes the value obvious — for a
   local business, often literally the search phrase to own ("emergency
   electrician Northside"). Category creation is a trap at this scale.

**The only-we rubric (lint for any positioning claim):** named against real
alternatives including "do nothing"? provable in one sentence? does an
identifiable segment care disproportionately? stated in the customer's
category language? Fail any → back up one rung.

The ladder feeds the value equation (§6): the alternatives are the reference
point, the unique attributes are the Belief proof stack, and the
who-cares-most segment is who the guarantee (7.2) is engineered for.

The full translation lives with each shape in `knowledge/industry-notes.md`

"Adapted" marks prescriptions translated for the shape rather than native to

| Shape | Usual true constraint at small scale | Lands directly | Handle with care |

| **Product / ecommerce** | Unit economics + differentiation (margin after fulfilment and CAC too thin, positioning too broad) | Money model at full strength (bumps, bundles, continuity, 30-day cash); volume doctrine applied to creative tests | Cash physics inverted (money leaves before it arrives) — judge by cash-conversion cycle; never scale ads before contribution margin per order is known |
| **Hospitality / walk-in** (adapted) | Repeat-visit rate, then off-peak utilization — not leads | LTV thinking (the regular IS the business); referral mechanics as bring-a-friend; behind-the-counter content | No pipeline — the "offer" is whatever earns visit two (loyalty mechanics, adapted); no countdown scarcity, no deep discounting (trains deal-hunters, poisons a habit business) |
| **Clinic / appointment** | Rebooking/recall rate and no-shows — the leaky bucket, not the tap | Activation + recall as retention math; speed-to-lead on enquiries; practitioner-referral partnerships | ALL proof through compliance: no outcome testimonials/before-afters in owned channels, no result guarantees, no urgency or price pressure on clinical decisions; sell logistics, never treatment |
| **Courses / community / coaching** | Audience/leads to the first ~$10k, then churn and delivery quality | Nearly everything at full strength — this is the source material's home turf | Practitioner-with-a-sideline: protect owner hours (8.3); competitive alternative is free content, so the only-we is proximity/accountability/specificity, never information; no income claims; no fake scarcity |
| **Software / digital product** (adapted) | Retention/product-fit wearing a marketing costume — check churn and activation before any lead prescription | LTGP:CAC discipline; trial as attraction offer; annual prepay as cash engineering; niche hard | Positioning ladder (§14) is the PRIMARY doctrine here; churn above ~5%/mo voids all growth spend; no discount-to-close |

**Show-rate note (adapted, for appointment-led shapes — clinics, brokers,
consults):** the booking is not the win; the show is. Remind hard (multiple
reminders, different channels), take deposits on long appointments, fill
cancellations from a standby list, and treat show-rate as a tracked funnel
stage. Directional planning constants: expect a meaningful minority of booked
strangers to no-show without reminders; deposits move it most.

**Referral & reward-inducement note (adapted — the canonical home for this
rule).** Regulated shapes can lawfully bar cash or outcome-tied referral rewards
paid to unlicensed parties (conflicted-remuneration / referral-fee rules).
Applies most sharply to **finance / mortgage broking, clinic / health, insurance,
and legal**; jurisdiction-specific real-estate referral-fee limits exist too.
For those shapes, do NOT design a cash-per-settled-referral or outcome-tied cash
inducement; use a non-cash thank-you, a service credit, or a charitable donation,
and tell the owner to check their own regulator. Non-regulated shapes (trades,
hospitality, most services) may use mutual cash/credit rewards freely. Any
referral or reward skill (`set-up-referrals`, proof/review work) keys off THIS
note rather than hardcoding its own list, so a newly-added shape inherits the
constraint.

## §17. The napkin-math library (compute out loud, in plain words)

These are the quick calculations that make a diagnosis land. Do them WITH the
owner, visibly:

- **CAC:** monthly acquisition spend ÷ new customers. ("Two grand on ads,
  eight new jobs — about $250 a job to win it.")
- **LTGP:** (revenue per period − delivery cost per period) × periods kept.
  Gross profit, never revenue.
- **The ratio verdict:** LTGP ÷ CAC → under 3 = fix the model; around 6 for a
  hands-on business = healthy; way above = spend more, not less.
- **Ceiling:** new-per-month ÷ churn-rate. ("Eight new a month, losing one in
  ten — you top out at 80 clients no matter what you spend.")
- **Churn payoff:** halving churn = doubling the ceiling, without one new
  lead.
- **Funnel-step leverage:** a 50% relative lift on the WORST stage (opt-in 8%
  → 12%, close 30% → 45%) grows the whole business by that lift with zero new
  traffic. Find the stage where that's plausible.
- **Price-rise maths:** at a 20% rise, how many customers could you LOSE and
  still make more? Run the margin arithmetic — the tolerable loss is usually
  bigger than the owner fears. Then the guarantee version: raise 20%, back it
  with a profit-back promise, keep the promise 8 times in 10, still ahead.
- **Owner-hours lens:** revenue ÷ owner hours = the owner's true rate; any
  recurring task below it is a delegation candidate (12.1).
- **Opportunity sizing (five minutes, always):** units × price × margin ×
  cycle time × capacity headroom → "can this make real money?" before any
  build.
- **Market-size sanity:** customers today ÷ addressable market → if
  penetration is tiny, the answer is depth in THIS market, not a new one.

## §18. Pain as a diagnostic input (and the boundary with shipped copy)

This doctrine uses pain as an ANALYTICAL input: the constraint rubric (§3), the
problem lists (7.1.2), and the value equation (§6) all read the customer's pain
to find the leverage. That analysis is internal. The boundary:

**Pain is a diagnostic input and a discovery-conversation tool. How the owner
frames the copy they SHIP to their customers (positive, pain-led, or any angle)
is the owner's choice, written in their own brand voice.** The guardrails that
DO bind any shipped copy (no em dashes, never invent evidence, no third-party
vendor) live in `knowledge/content-rules.md`; positive-only is not imposed on a
client's marketing.

Applications: problem lists (7.1.2) live in internal worksheets; the offer's
five naming parts (7.5), hooks, ad callouts, diagnostic magnets, urgency, and
guarantees are all built from this analysis, then written in the owner's voice
and framing. Live 1:1 discovery conversations name pain in the customer's own
words (the discovery-arc boundary, 12.5).
