# YouTube Packaging Method — how a video earns the click

The canonical knowledge for the "packaging" half of the YouTube factory: the
idea, the angle, the title, and the thumbnail concept, decided together before a
word is scripted. This is the operator-facing method; the skills that use it stay
lean and reference this file:

- [`research-my-channel`](../skills/research-my-channel/SKILL.md) builds the
  gap-and-angle map this file describes (the competitor scan + comment mining +
  novel-packaging read).
- [`plan-my-youtube`](../skills/plan-my-youtube/SKILL.md) turns that map into a
  pipeline: per video an idea → angle → working title → thumbnail concept.
- [`youtube-thumbnail-method.md`](youtube-thumbnail-method.md) owns the thumbnail
  *craft* (layout, headline rules, hero families). This file owns the *concept*
  that thumbnail then executes. Don't restate one in the other.

**Packaging is the title and the thumbnail, decided as a pair.** On YouTube the
video is not the product until someone clicks. The title and thumbnail are the
product a viewer actually chooses between, in a fraction of a second, against a
wall of other choices. A good video with weak packaging stays unwatched; that is
the single most common way a channel stalls. So packaging is decided first, and
the script is written to deliver on the promise the packaging made.

When in doubt, this file wins over instinct. Instinct says "describe the video".
Packaging says "make the click the obvious choice, then keep the promise".

## Outlier analysis — read against the channel's own baseline, not the whole platform

The one measurement that matters is not raw views. It is **how a video performed
relative to what that channel normally does.** A video with 40,000 views on a
channel that averages 5,000 is an *outlier* — the format, angle, or packaging did
something the channel's usual videos don't. A video with 200,000 views on a
channel that averages 500,000 is an under-performer, even though the number is
bigger. Raw view count mostly measures how big the channel already is. The
outlier multiple measures what *worked*.

**How to spot an outlier keyless (from a channel's public video list):**

| Signal | What it tells you |
|---|---|
| Views far above the channel's recent median | The packaging or angle beat the channel's baseline. Study it. |
| A recent video already past an older video's total | It is compounding fast (a strong outlier), even young. |
| One title/thumbnail shape repeating across a creator's top videos | That shape is their proven pattern. It is copyable at the *structure* level. |
| An old video still gaining views | It is "evergreen" — the topic has standing search/suggested demand. |

Estimate the baseline from the channel's recent uploads (the median of the last
10–20 videos you can see), then flag anything that clears it by a clear multiple.
You are looking for the *why*: was it the topic, the angle, the title promise, or
the thumbnail? Name the reason, because the reason is what transfers to the
owner's channel. A view count you can't explain teaches nothing.

**Look across the niche, not just one channel.** The strongest signal is the same
angle out-performing on *several* channels. That is a niche-wide demand you can
serve, not one creator's fluke.

## The three levers of differentiation — idea, angle, packaging

A video is chosen on three things, in this order of leverage:

**1. The idea (the topic).** The single biggest lever. A great title on a topic
nobody wants still loses. Idea selection comes from the research: topics the niche
proves people want (outliers, repeated coverage, and the questions viewers ask in
comments), filtered to the ones the owner can speak to with real authority.

**2. The angle (the fresh take on a proven idea).** This is where most of the win
lives, and where most owners under-invest. A proven topic packaged the *same* way
as everyone else competes on channel size alone, which a new channel loses. The
same topic with an angle nobody is taking is winnable from zero. Angle is "what is
different about *how I approach this*".

- ✅ Proven idea "how to quote a job", fresh angle: *"How I Quote a Job in Under a Minute"* (speed + a specific, testable claim)
- ❌ Proven idea "how to quote a job", tired angle: *"Quoting Tips for Tradies"* (the exact shape ten bigger channels already own)

**3. The packaging (title + thumbnail).** The execution of the angle. Same craft
rules as the thumbnail method for the thumbnail; title rules below.

The differentiation questions to run on every planned video:

- What is the *proven* version of this idea in the niche (the outlier to learn from)?
- What angle on it is nobody else taking?
- Can the title say the outcome plainly, and can the thumbnail show it in one glance?
- Does the owner have real authority or a real result to back the promise?

## Title rules for a real channel (not a tutorial index)

Titles here are the *owner's* channel titles, in the owner's brand and voice. They
are not a software tutorial index. The job is the click, backed by a promise the
video keeps.

**Lead with the outcome or the intrigue, not a dry label.**
- ✅ *"How I Quote a Job in Under a Minute"*
- ❌ *"Quoting Process Overview"*

**Make a specific, concrete promise. Specific out-pulls vague, every time.**
- ✅ *"The 3 Questions That Win Me Every Job"*
- ❌ *"Some Tips for Winning More Work"*

**Numbers and timeframes make a promise testable, which makes it clickable.**
- ✅ *"I Cut My Admin to 20 Minutes a Day"*
- ✅ *"5 Jobs I Turned Down (and Why That Grew My Business)"*

**Curiosity is a promise too — but it must be honest and paid off in the video.**
A title that teases something the video never delivers trains viewers to stop
trusting the channel, and YouTube reads the early exits. Tease the real thing.
- ✅ *"The One Quoting Change That Won Me a Whole Year Back"* (real story, paid off)
- ❌ *"You Won't BELIEVE What Happened Next"* (empty tease, exit-bait)

**Keep it legible on a phone.** Most watch time is mobile, where roughly the first
40–50 characters are what a viewer actually reads before it truncates. Front-load
the promise into those first few words.

**Match the searcher's words for how-to and evergreen topics.** For a topic people
actively search ("how to price a paint job"), the title should contain the words
they type. For browse/suggested topics (stories, opinions, results), intrigue and
specificity matter more than search-match.

## Franchise thinking — a repeatable format beats a lucky hit

A single viral video is a spike. A **franchise** is a repeatable format the
channel can run again and again, so each new episode compounds on the last one's
audience. This is how channels grow predictably instead of hoping to get lucky.

A franchise is a title-and-format *template* with a swappable variable:

- *"I Quoted a [Job Type] in Under a Minute"* → episode 2 is a different job type
- *"The Real Cost of [Common Service]"* → a whole series, one service per video
- *"[Number] Things the Best [Trade]s Always Do"* → refreshable every year

Why it works:
- **The viewer knows what they're getting**, so a proven format earns the click faster each time.
- **YouTube learns the pattern** and suggests new episodes to people who watched the last one.
- **The owner gets a reusable production lane** instead of inventing a new video from scratch every week.

When planning a pipeline, prefer ideas that can become a franchise over one-off
topics. One strong repeatable format is worth more than five unrelated videos. The
`plan-my-youtube` pipeline should name the franchise a video belongs to (or mark
it a standalone) so the channel builds toward a small set of proven lanes.

## Building the novel-packaging gap-and-angle map

This is the deliverable `research-my-channel` produces and `plan-my-youtube`
consumes. It is built from what real competitor videos and real viewer comments
show, never from invention. Three moves:

**1. Map what's already packaged (and how).** List the niche's out-performing
videos with their title shape and thumbnail concept. Cluster the ones packaged
*identically* — a cluster means that shape is proven *and* crowded. A new channel
entering a crowded shape competes on size and loses.

**2. Find the untaken angles.** For each proven idea, ask what angle nobody in the
cluster is taking: a different audience ("for solo operators", "for someone doing
this the first time"), a contrarian take, a real-result story, a specific
timeframe or number, the owner's own hard-won lesson. An untaken angle on a proven
idea is the single best packaging opportunity a small channel has.

**3. Mine the comments for demand in the audience's own words.** The comments on
top videos are a free demand signal. Look for:
- Repeated questions the videos didn't answer ("but what about…") — each is a video idea.
- "Nobody explains X" / "I wish someone would show…" — an explicit gap, stated by the viewer.
- Confusion or disagreement — a chance to be the clearer, more honest voice.

**Every idea on the map carries a verbatim-evidence line** — the real comment or
the real observed outlier it rests on. An idea with no evidence is a guess, and
guesses get marked as guesses, never dressed up as findings. This is the same
safeguard the research and site-builder floors carry: the audience's own words, or
a real observed video, back every claim.

## Banned framings (packaging edition)

- **Empty clickbait** — teasing something the video won't deliver. It buys one
  click and loses the viewer's trust plus the retention signal. Tease the real thing.
- **Invented outliers or fabricated comments** — never cite a view count, a
  competitor result, or an audience quote you did not actually observe. If it isn't
  in the research, it isn't on the map.
- **Copying a title word-for-word** — learn the *structure* of a proven shape,
  never lift the exact title. Bring the owner's own angle.
- **Third-party vendor names in the owner's packaging** — the owner's brand and
  voice, not a tool's name, unless the video is genuinely about that tool.
- **Vague, everyone-else titles** — "Tips and Tricks", "My Thoughts On…",
  "Update Video". They make no promise, so they earn no click.
- **Negative or fear-led framing in owner-facing titles** — lead with the win the
  viewer gets, not the pain they're in.

## Common mistakes (don't re-walk these)

| Mistake | Fix |
|---|---|
| Ranking ideas by raw view count | Rank by the outlier multiple against each channel's own baseline |
| A proven topic packaged the same as ten bigger channels | Keep the topic, bring an angle nobody in the cluster is taking |
| Title describes the video instead of promising an outcome | Lead with the outcome or an honest, paid-off intrigue |
| Promise is vague ("some tips") | Make it specific and testable — a number, a timeframe, a concrete result |
| Title too long to read on a phone | Front-load the promise into the first ~40 characters |
| One-off video ideas with nothing in common | Group them into a franchise — a repeatable format with one swappable variable |
| An idea on the map with no evidence behind it | Attach the real comment or observed outlier, or mark it a guess |
| Thumbnail concept invented in isolation from the title | Decide title + thumbnail as a pair; they make one promise together |

## Output rule

Everything the owner reads (the research map, the pipeline, the title options)
follows the positive-only rule and uses no em dashes (use commas, colons,
parentheses, or separate sentences). Frame the owner's own opportunity by what
they can go win, never by what their channel "lacks". A rival's crowded packaging
or an unanswered comment is fair as a sharp observation about the gap; the owner's
position is always the fresh angle they're about to take.
