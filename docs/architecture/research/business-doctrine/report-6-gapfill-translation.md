# GAP-FILLERS AND TRANSLATION MAP — Research Report

Research basis: Dunford's published quickstart guide and Obviously Awesome summaries, the MIT/InsideSales lead-response study, Google LSA/GBP ranking analyses, contractor/tradie pricing doctrine (Joist, Fergus, Housecall Pro, Tradies Success Academy), Jonathan Stark's capacity-pricing doctrine, restaurant retention economics (Olo/Paytronix/Restroworks), and AHPRA advertising rules (relevant because the BOS ships from an Australian company and clinic operators face equivalents in most markets). All synthesized in my own words. The repo's existing `knowledge/industry-notes.md` shape structure (pipeline model / reliefs / gotchas / comms style) was read and the translation map below is written to slot into that structure as a new per-shape block.

## A. FRAMEWORKS

### A1. Positioning Ladder (source: April Dunford, "Obviously Awesome")

Dunford's method, compressed to an executable procedure. Her core insight: positioning is an ordered derivation, not brainstorming. Start anywhere but competitive alternatives and you get neat-but-undifferentiated positioning.

**Procedure (strict order):**
1. **List competitive alternatives** — what the customer would actually do if you didn't exist. Critically, this includes "do nothing," "spreadsheet/DIY," "cheap generalist," and "hire a person," not just named competitors. Rule: exclude "phantom competitors" that never actually appear in real deals; only list what customers genuinely weigh you against. For a small service business, "my brother-in-law will do it" and "put it off another year" are usually alternatives #1 and #2.
2. **Isolate unique attributes** — features, capabilities, or facts (speed, guarantee, credential, locality, process) that you have and the listed alternatives don't. Must be provable, not aspirational.
3. **Translate attributes to value** — for each attribute, ask "so what?" twice until you reach a customer outcome. Cluster into 1-3 value themes.
4. **Find who cares most** — the customer characteristics that make someone care disproportionately about those value themes. This is the target segment; it is derived, not chosen by preference.
5. **Pick the market category** — the frame of reference that makes the value obvious. Dunford: position in an *existing* category by default (roughly 90% of successful tech companies did); category creation is a trap for small operators. For a local business the "category" is often the Google search phrase they should own ("emergency electrician Northside," not "electrical solutions provider").
6. *(Her 5+1)* **Layer relevant trends** only if genuinely load-bearing; never lead with a trend.

**Composes with Hormozi:** Dunford answers the question Hormozi's Grand Slam Offer assumes is already answered: *against what, and for whom exactly?* The value equation (dream outcome × perceived likelihood ÷ time × effort) needs a reference point; competitive alternatives ARE the reference point. Run the Positioning Ladder first, then build the Grand Slam Offer inside the chosen frame: unique attributes become the proof stack for "perceived likelihood of achievement," and the "who cares most" segment is who the offer's guarantee and risk-reversal should be engineered for. Hormozi's "niche down" advice becomes rigorous: the niche is derived from step 4, not guessed. The BOS's `build-brand-strategy` "only-we claim" should be defined as: an attribute from step 2 that survives the test "can any listed alternative say this truthfully?"

**Rubric for the only-we claim (usable as a lint):**
- Named against real alternatives, including "do nothing"?
- Provable within one sentence (number, guarantee, credential, mechanism)?
- Does at least one identifiable customer segment care disproportionately?
- Stated in the customer's category language, not an invented category?
Fail any → back up one rung of the ladder.

### A2. Local Gravity Stack (source: local-services doctrine — Google LSA/GBP ranking analyses, MIT lead-response study)

For any business bought via local search or word-of-mouth (trades, clinics, hospitality, many services), marketing effort has a strict priority order. Doctrine-level, platform-agnostic:

**Priority order (do not advance a tier until the one above is solid):**
1. **Answer speed.** The MIT/InsideSales study: contacting a lead within 5 minutes vs 30 minutes improves contact odds ~100x and qualification odds ~21x. For local services this dominates everything; the first business to respond wins a large share of jobs regardless of price. Missed-call text-back and instant enquiry acknowledgment are tier-one infrastructure, not automation nice-to-haves.
2. **Review engine.** Review count, recency, and rating are the strongest visibility signal in local search/LSA placement and the strongest trust signal at decision time. The doctrine is *velocity, not stock*: a steady drip (reviews from the last 90 days) beats a large stale total. The ask must be systematic — triggered at the moment of demonstrated satisfaction (job complete, result delivered), by the person who did the work, with a direct link. Reply to every review; replies are read by the *next* customer, not the reviewer.
3. **Profile completeness and local basics.** Accurate categories, services, photos, hours, service area; consistent name/address/phone across the web. One-time fix plus quarterly hygiene. This is a floor, not a lever.
4. **Proof publishing.** Before/after photos, job stories, case studies mapped to the searches people make. This is the local version of Hormozi's "document, don't create."
5. **Community presence.** Sponsorships, partnerships with adjacent trades/referrers, local groups. Real but slow; a multiplier on tiers 1-4, worthless without them.
6. **Paid local (ads/LSA).** Last, because paid traffic amplifies whatever the tiers above are: fast-responding well-reviewed businesses get cheaper leads and higher close rates; slow ones burn the budget.

**Composes with Hormozi:** This is the Core Four (warm outreach, content, cold outreach, ads) re-weighted for local. Hormozi's engine assumes lead flow is the constraint and effort volume is the lever; locally, the *conversion infrastructure* (speed + reviews) is usually the constraint, and it's cheap to fix. Rule of thumb for the diagnosis method: a local business gets no new lead-generation prescription until (a) enquiries are answered inside 5 minutes during business hours and (b) a review ask fires on every completed job. Hormozi's "give away free value" translates locally as the free quote/assessment done fast and generously, which itself feeds tiers 1 and 2.

### A3. Owner-Hour Economics (source: micro-business economics — Jonathan Stark's capacity pricing, effective-rate studies, synthesized)

For sub-$500k solo and 2-3 person operators, the scarcest unit is not cash or leads: it is *owner hours*, because the owner is simultaneously the salesperson, the delivery mechanism, and the admin department. Frameworks:

**Effective Owner Rate (EOR):** last 90 days' owner profit ÷ total hours the owner worked (all hours, not billable hours). Professional-services research shows operators who believe they charge $200/hr commonly realize $90-130 after write-downs, non-billable time, and collection gaps; small firms bill only 55-70% of worked time. The EOR is the BOS's honest baseline number. Every prescribed move should state its expected effect on EOR.

**The Capacity Rule:** when the owner is at or near delivery capacity (calendar full, lead flow steady), the correct move is *price*, not *volume*. Raising prices when fully booked either lifts EOR directly or sheds the least-profitable work and recovers capacity, and price becomes the filter that quote-chasers select themselves out on. This is the single most important inversion of Hormozi for this audience.

**Adapting "do 100x more":** Hormozi's volume doctrine (Rule of 100: 100 outreaches/100 minutes of content per day) assumes the doer's time is cheap relative to the upside. When the owner IS delivery, the adaptation is: **volume through systems, not hours.** The daily unit becomes "100 units of *automated or delegated* outreach" (review asks, follow-up sequences, reactivation messages the system sends), plus a small fixed owner ration (e.g., 5 warm outreaches or 1 content piece/day) protected as an appointment. The BOS is itself the mechanism: Claude does the 100x, the owner does the 5.

**Owner-hour triage (weekly):** classify the owner's week into (1) only-the-owner work — selling, delivering the rare-skill core, key relationships; (2) system-able work — quoting, follow-up, invoicing, scheduling, content drafting; (3) deletable work. Prescription order: delete 3, systematize 2, protect and price up 1. A solo operator's "hiring plan" starts with buying back hours via systems at near-zero cost before any payroll.

**Composes with Hormozi:** Hormozi's constraint framing (leads / sales / delivery / profit) stays, but for micro-businesses add a fifth constraint that frequently dominates: **owner capacity**. His "more, better, new" ordering holds, with "better" (pricing, conversion, systemization) usually beating "more" (volume) whenever the capacity rule triggers. His LTV/CAC math still governs; EOR is the micro-scale proxy when the owner can't yet compute LTGP cleanly.

### A4. Quote-Market Pricing (source: trades/contractor pricing doctrine — Joist, Fergus, Housecall Pro, value-pricing consultants)

How premium pricing coexists with quote-comparison markets (trades, clinics with gap fees, any "get three quotes" purchase). Value-based pricing survives comparison shopping only if the quote itself changes the comparison:

**Procedure:**
1. **Never quote a bare number.** A number alone forces comparison on price only. Every quote carries: what's included (itemized inclusions), the mechanism (how you'll do it and why that way), risk reversal (warranty, guarantee, tidy-site/turn-up-on-time promise), and proof (review count, relevant job photos).
2. **Three-tier the quote (good/better/best).** Tiering moves the buyer's question from "you vs. the other quotes" to "which of your options," raises average ticket, and reduces pressure to match the lowest bid. The middle tier is the intended sale; the bottom tier is the price-anchor that competes with the cheap quote while remaining profitable.
3. **Price from cost floor + value ceiling.** Compute the true cost floor (materials, labor at loaded rates, overhead allocation, callback risk); set the price by the value ceiling (urgency, consequence of failure, buyer's alternative cost) — never below floor, positioned within the local band's *upper* half once tiers 1-2 of the Local Gravity Stack are in place.
4. **Speed is part of the price.** In quote-comparison markets the first professional-looking quote often wins at a 10-20% premium because the buyer wants the decision over with. Same-day quoting is a pricing strategy, not just an ops win.
5. **Follow the quote.** Quotes decay in days; a structured 2-3 touch follow-up is where a large share of wins actually happen (this matches the existing trades gotcha in industry-notes).
6. **Let the bottom go.** Structured pricing intentionally loses lowest-price shoppers; doctrine treats that as filtration, not failure.

**Composes with Hormozi:** This is the Grand Slam Offer ported into a quote document: the tiers are his value-stack and price-anchor logic; the guarantee is his risk reversal; the inclusion list is "perceived likelihood." Where Hormozi says "charge so much it feels uncomfortable," quote-market doctrine adds the constraint: the premium must be *legible inside the quote* the buyer puts next to two others. The BOS's `price-my-work` and `write-a-proposal` should treat the quote as an offer-presentation surface, not a number-delivery surface.

### A5. Regulated-Trust Marketing (source: AHPRA and equivalent professional-conduct regimes, synthesized to doctrine level)

For clinics and regulated professionals, an entire band of Hormozi tactics is legally or ethically off-limits, and the doctrine must say so *before* prescribing:
- **No outcome testimonials in advertising** (AHPRA s133: patient stories referencing symptoms, treatments, or outcomes are prohibited in anything the clinic controls, including its own social pages). Reviews praising service, friendliness, wait times, and booking ease are fine. So: run the review engine (A2) but harvest *experience* language, never *clinical-outcome* language, in owned channels.
- **No guarantees of clinical results, no urgency/scarcity pressure on health decisions, no discount-driven inducements for regulated services** (varies by regime, but the safe doctrine is: never let price pressure or time pressure touch a clinical decision).
- The compliant substitutes: practitioner credentials and mechanism explanation (allowed), education content, availability/access speed, experience-quality proof, and recall/rebooking systems (retention, where the real economics live anyway).

**Composes with Hormozi:** the value equation still applies, but "perceived likelihood of achievement" must be built from credentials, mechanism, and volume-of-experience rather than testimonials and guarantees; and money-model aggression (upsells, scarcity) is redirected from the clinical decision to the *logistics* layer (package pre-booking of an already-clinically-indicated care plan is fine; pressure to accept more treatment is not).

## B. TRANSLATION MAP — per-shape briefs

Format per shape: **Lands directly / Adapt how / Traps / Most common true constraint at small scale.**

### B1. Service / professional (brokers, consultants)
- **Lands directly:** Grand Slam Offer construction (these buyers evaluate written proposals, the natural offer surface); CLOSER sales framework on discovery calls; warm outreach as the #1 lead channel; the referral ask at the moment of a win; "charge premium, deliver certainty."
- **Adapt how:** "Do 100x more volume" → volume through the follow-up system, not owner hours (A3). Content doctrine → proof-of-expertise content (case breakdowns, market commentary) rather than mass-reach content; 1-2 strong pieces/week beats daily noise. Guarantees → often can't guarantee outcomes (broker can't guarantee approval); substitute service-level guarantees (response time, delivery date, "we handle everything") per A5 logic. Positioning: run the full Dunford ladder; "trusted advisor" is not positioning, the competitive alternative is usually "go direct to the bank / big firm / DIY," and the only-we claim must beat *that*.
- **Traps:** Aggressive scarcity/urgency on advice-based decisions (destroys the trust the whole model runs on); discounting to win (anchors the relationship at the wrong altitude); paid cold traffic before referral/renewal systems exist; regulated verticals (finance, insurance) inherit A5 constraints — never "guarantee," never imply outcomes.
- **True constraint at small scale:** almost always **sales-cycle leakage**, not leads — proposals stalling in "sent," referral asks never made, renewals not surfaced. Fix follow-through before touching lead gen.

### B2. Trades / on-the-tools
- **Lands directly:** Value equation as quote logic (time-to-done and effort/sacrifice are exactly what premium trades buyers pay for); risk-reversal guarantees (workmanship warranty, turn-up-on-time); "raise prices" — most underpriced segment in the whole map; lead magnet as free quote/assessment done fast.
- **Adapt how:** Core Four → replace with the Local Gravity Stack order (A2): answer speed, reviews, profile, proof photos, then ads. Offer presentation → the three-tier quote (A4), because the buying ritual is quote comparison. Volume doctrine → the owner can't do outreach from a roof; the system does the 100x (missed-call text-back, quote chase, review asks). Upsell doctrine → lands as maintenance plans and "while we're there" add-ons quoted upfront in the "best" tier, not pressure at invoice time.
- **Traps:** Hormozi-style aggressive close scripts (kills word-of-mouth in a reputation-dense local market); scaling ad spend while calls ring out (paying for leads you already lose); discounting to win comparison quotes (below-floor work plus the worst customers); "hire setters/closers" prescriptions — irrelevant below ~$1M.
- **True constraint at small scale:** **response speed and quote follow-through** — jobs are lost before pricing is ever the issue. Second most common: pricing below the cost floor while believing they're competitive.

### B3. Product-seller / ecommerce-retail
- **Lands directly:** Money-model doctrine at nearly full strength — this is the shape closest to Hormozi's native math: AOV/LTV optimization, bumps, bundles, post-purchase upsells, subscribe-and-save continuity, client-financed acquisition (first-order economics funding ads); "more better new" on ad creative volume.
- **Adapt how:** Volume doctrine applies to *creative and SKU tests*, not owner outreach. Dunford ladder is critical here: a small product seller cannot win a category, so step 5 (market category) must narrow to a sub-category or use-case they can own ("running socks for trail ultras," not "socks"). Retention doctrine → email/SMS flows and repeat-purchase rate are the profit engine; review velocity (A2 tier 2) transfers to product reviews.
- **Traps:** Scaling paid ads before contribution margin per order is known (the fastest way a micro e-com dies); competing on price against platforms; deep-discount launches that train the list to wait; inventory bets driven by revenue targets instead of cash-conversion cycle (this shape's cash physics differ from every service shape — money leaves *before* it arrives).
- **True constraint at small scale:** **unit economics and differentiation** — margin after fulfillment and CAC is too thin to fund growth, usually because positioning (step 5) is too broad. It's the one shape where leads are genuinely bought, so the constraint is whether the math survives buying them.

### B4. Hospitality / walk-in (cafe, restaurant, salon-adjacent walk-in)
- **Lands directly:** LTV thinking (the regular is the business: repeat customers drive ~60-70% of revenue in most formats; a 5% retention lift moves profit 25%+); referral mechanics as "bring a friend" and word-of-mouth; "document don't create" as behind-the-counter content.
- **Adapt how:** Offers → not Grand Slam Offers but *first-visit conversion to second visit*: the doctrinal number is that ~70% of first-time diners never return, so the highest-leverage "offer" is whatever earns visit #2 (loyalty stamp, name-remembered welcome, next-visit hook). Lead gen → Local Gravity Stack; Maps presence and review velocity are the acquisition engine, ads mostly aren't. Continuity doctrine → loyalty program (members visit ~20% more and spend ~20% more), not subscriptions.
- **Traps:** Scarcity/urgency tactics and hard upsells (a cafe running countdown timers reads desperate and poisons a habit business); Groupon-style deep discounting (buys one-time deal hunters at negative margin, trains regulars to expect discounts); chasing new-customer volume while the retention floor leaks; heavy CRM/pipeline prescriptions — walk-in businesses have no pipeline, so BOS skills built on opportunity stages need a "no-pipeline mode" here.
- **True constraint at small scale:** **repeat-visit rate** (retention), then **capacity utilization at off-peak** — not leads. The prescription set is loyalty mechanics, consistency of product, and filling quiet hours with community/regulars, not funnels.

### B5. Clinic / appointment (physio, dental, allied health, vet-adjacent)
- **Lands directly:** LTV via care-plan completion and recall (the economics live in rebooking, exactly Hormozi's retention math); speed-to-lead on new-patient enquiries (a missed call is a patient who booked elsewhere); referral relationships with adjacent practitioners.
- **Adapt how:** All proof doctrine passes through A5: reviews harvested for *experience* language, credentials and mechanism replace testimonials and guarantees. Offers → packaged care plans presented as clinical recommendations with clear pricing, never discount-driven bundles. Upsell → "recall and rebook before they leave the room" is the compliant, high-leverage version. Show-rate doctrine (reminders, deposits for long appointments) is the clinic's version of sales-conversion optimization.
- **Traps:** Outcome testimonials, before/afters, and result guarantees in owned channels (regulator breach, real fines); scarcity pressure on treatment decisions; aggressive treatment upsells (clinical-ethics violation and reputation killer); time-limited discounts on regulated services.
- **True constraint at small scale:** **rebooking/recall rate and no-shows** — the leaky bucket, not the tap. New-patient marketing is only the constraint for brand-new clinics; for established ones the diagnosis should check recall compliance and cancellation fill-rate first.

### B6. Courses / community / coaching
- **Lands directly:** Essentially Hormozi's home turf — nearly everything: Grand Slam Offer, value equation, Core Four at full volume (the owner's content time IS delivery-adjacent), money models (attraction offers, ascension, continuity/community), Rule of 100, guarantees and risk reversal, "make more offers."
- **Adapt how:** The BOS audience version is usually a *practitioner* adding a course/community sideline, not a media business: protect the EOR rule (A3) so content volume doesn't cannibalize the core business's delivery hours; start with one offer and one channel, not the full Core Four. Dunford ladder matters because "course about X" is an oversaturated category; the competitive alternative is free YouTube, so the only-we claim must be about proximity, accountability, or specificity, never information.
- **Traps:** Fake scarcity ("doors closing" that reopen weekly — audiences now punish it); income-claim marketing (regulatory exposure and trust damage); building the course before validating with a paid pilot; continuity pricing that outruns delivered value (churn cliff at month 3).
- **True constraint at small scale:** **audience/leads for the first $10k, then churn/delivery quality after** — the one shape where Hormozi's "you need more volume" diagnosis is usually literally correct early on.

### B7. Software / digital product
- **Lands directly:** LTV:CAC discipline; churn as the master metric; free-trial/freemium as the attraction offer; annual-plan prepay as cash-flow engineering (client-financed acquisition); niching down hard.
- **Adapt how:** This is where Dunford is the *primary* doctrine, not the gap-filler — she wrote it for this shape. The full ladder, with "existing category by default" enforced. Hormozi's sales doctrine adapts to founder-led demos with CLOSER structure; his volume doctrine adapts to outbound + content in a tight niche rather than broad ads. Pricing doctrine → value-metric pricing (price scales with the value unit) replaces per-seat reflexes.
- **Traps:** Scaling paid acquisition pre-retention (Hormozi's own caveat, but fatal faster in SaaS: churn above ~5%/mo makes every marketing prescription void); aggressive discount-to-close (destroys expansion revenue and price integrity); "hire a sales team" moves below ~$1M ARR; category creation (Dunford's explicit warning).
- **True constraint at small scale:** **retention/product-market fit masquerading as a marketing problem.** Diagnosis rule: check churn and activation before any lead prescription; if churn is high, all growth spend is deferred.
