# DOMAIN REPORT: OFFERS AND VALUE (Hormozi / $100M Offers)

## A. FRAMEWORKS

### A1. The Value Equation (core scoring rubric)

**Formula:** Value = (Dream Outcome × Perceived Likelihood of Achievement) ÷ (Time Delay × Effort & Sacrifice)

Two numerator variables to raise, two denominator variables to drive toward zero. Critical doctrine points:
- Every variable is **perceived**, not actual. The offer only becomes valuable when the prospect *perceives* the likelihood, *perceives* the speed, *perceives* the ease. Marketing work is perception work; fulfilment work is actuality work. Both matter, in that order.
- The denominator is the higher-leverage side. Hormozi's stated ideal: if you could make the denominator zero (instant, effortless), value approaches infinite and you can charge almost anything. Most small operators compete on the numerator (bigger promises); the money is in the denominator (faster, easier).
- "Fast beats free." A slower cheaper option loses to a faster dearer one for most buyers in pain.

**Scoring procedure (0-5 each, run on any offer):**
1. Dream Outcome: does the offer name the destination the buyer actually pictures (status, feeling, end-state), or the vehicle/deliverable? 0 = names the deliverable ("6 sessions of physio"), 5 = names the arrival ("back on the netball court by March").
2. Perceived Likelihood: what proof, guarantee, specificity, and "people exactly like me" evidence is in the offer? 0 = bare claim, 5 = named guarantee + local proof + specific mechanism.
3. Time Delay: how long until the buyer *feels* a first win? 0 = months and invisible, 5 = an engineered win inside days of paying.
4. Effort & Sacrifice: what does the buyer still have to do, learn, give up, or organise? 0 = heavy DIY, 5 = done-for-you.
- **Stop rule:** any variable scoring 0-1 is the constraint. Fix that one before touching the others. Do not raise price until Likelihood ≥3 (a premium price on a low-certainty offer just raises refunds and resentment).

**How to raise each variable (the playbook):**
- Dream Outcome: don't inflate the promise, sharpen it. Restate it as the buyer's own words, and frame it in status terms (what others will see/say). Sell the vacation, not the plane flight.
- Likelihood: add proof (reviews, before/afters, counts of jobs done), add a guarantee, narrow the avatar (a program "for shift nurses" is more believable to a shift nurse than a generic one), show the mechanism.
- Time Delay: engineer a fast emotional win close to purchase (the "first win" milestone), shorten onboarding, give something usable on day one. Buyers stay for the long-term outcome because of short-term experience.
- Effort & Sacrifice: move items from do-it-yourself toward done-with-you/done-for-you; remove steps, decisions, and homework; done-for-you always prices above DIY for the same outcome.

**Why it works:** it converts "is this a good offer?" from taste into four independently fixable levers, and it explains why two offers with identical fulfilment sell at 100x different prices.

### A2. Market First: the Starving Crowd test (pre-offer gate)

**Hierarchy:** Starving Crowd (market) > Offer Strength > Persuasion Skills. A great market forgives a mediocre offer; no offer saves a dead market. Run this gate BEFORE any offer work.

**Score the market on four indicators (pass/fail each):**
1. **Massive pain** — they desperately need it, not merely want it. "The pain is the pitch." The degree of pain is proportional to the price you can charge.
2. **Purchasing power** — the audience can actually pay the price that makes the business work.
3. **Easy to target** — there is a findable channel/watering hole (a trade association, a suburb, a Facebook group, a searcher intent).
4. **Growing** — growing markets are tailwind; declining ones are headwind. "Normal" (market-rate growth) is acceptable.
- **Stop rule:** any hard fail on pain or purchasing power → reposition the avatar before writing a single offer word. All markets ladder up to health, wealth, or relationships; if you can't trace the offer to one of those, the pain is probably too weak.

**Why it works:** demand is channelled, not created. Most small-business "offer problems" are actually avatar problems.

### A3. Niche Commitment and the Niche Slap

- **The niche slap:** a newer operator half-heartedly tries one offer in one market, doesn't win, and wrongly concludes "bad market." The prescription is commit, then iterate offers *within* the niche; if you try a hundred offers in one niche you will find one that hits. Switching niches restarts the learning clock to zero every time.
- **Riches in niches, with a size threshold:** under roughly $10M/year revenue, niching down makes you MORE money, not less. (Every 2-10 person business in BOS is far under this line, so for BOS users the rule is unconditional: narrower.)
- **The 100x pricing proof:** the same core product priced by specificity: generic course $19 → "for sales professionals" $99 → "for B2B outbound reps" $499 → "for B2B outbound reps selling to [specific industry]" $1,997ish. Same content, different avatar-specificity, ~100x price. (Book example; wording varies across retellings, magnitude is consistent.)
- **Niching ladder (procedure):** "I solve [problem]" → "for [specific person]" → "in [unique, counterintuitive way] that reverses their deepest fear." Each rung earns price.
- **Stop rule:** do not broaden the niche because of boredom or a slow fortnight. Broaden only when the niche is genuinely saturated by YOU (you are the known name in it) or you clear the revenue threshold.

**Why it works:** specificity raises Perceived Likelihood ("this was built for someone exactly like me"), which is a numerator lever you get for free, without changing fulfilment.

### A4. Grand Slam Offer construction (the 5-step build)

Goal: an offer so differentiated it can't be price-compared, sold in a "category of one," so the decision becomes your offer vs nothing, not your offer vs the cheaper competitor.

1. **Identify the Dream Outcome.** Not the membership, the weight loss. Not the flight, the vacation. Write it as an arrival scene in the buyer's words.
2. **List every problem** between the buyer and that outcome. Walk the buyer's timeline: immediately before purchase, during, immediately after, and the "next thing" after each step. For each step, generate problems through the four value-driver lenses (it won't work for me / too slow / too hard / not worth it). Insane detail wins; a small list means you stopped early.
3. **Turn each problem into a solution statement.** Mechanical transform: prepend "how to" and reverse the problem ("shopping healthy is confusing" → "how to make buying healthy food easy so anyone can do it"). This produces the checklist of everything the offer must deliver.
4. **Brainstorm delivery vehicles for each solution** (divergent thinking, no filtering yet). Use the cheat-code prompts: one-on-one vs small group vs one-to-many; level of personal attention; effort expected from them; live vs recorded; response time; environment. Two forcing questions: *"If my customer paid me 10x, what would I deliver?"* and *"If they paid me 1/10th, how would I have to deliver it to still get them the result?"* The 10x answer reveals premium tiers; the 1/10th answer reveals scalable assets.
5. **Trim & Stack.** Trim: cut high-cost/low-value items first, then low-cost/low-value. Keep high-value/low-cost (especially one-to-many assets: templates, checklists, recorded trainings, software; high one-time creation cost, near-zero marginal cost). Stack: present what remains as a named bundle of components, each with its own value, whose sum dwarfs the price.
- **Completeness rule:** the final bundle must solve **every** perceived problem, not most. One unsolved problem becomes the reason the prospect doesn't buy. Don't get romantic about how you'd prefer to solve it.
- **Bundle test (all three or rework):** (a) solves all perceived problems; (b) you genuinely believe it is one of a kind; (c) it is impossible to compare to the shop down the street.

**Why it works:** it converts an offer from a commodity line-item ("pay us, we do work") into a differentiated system, which flips the purchase from price-driven to value-driven while fulfilment cost barely moves.

### A5. Pricing doctrine: charge more, and the virtuous cycle of price

- **Price-to-value divergence:** people buy when perceived value exceeds price by a wide margin. You win by *widening the gap*, and there are two ways: cut price (race to the bottom) or grow value (the whole point of A4). Rule: "never charge more than your product is worth, but charge far more than it costs to fulfil."
- **The default pricing anti-pattern (what NOT to do):** look at the market → average competitors → price slightly below → offer "a little more" → arrive at "more for less." The competitors being copied are broke.
- **The virtuous cycle of raising price:** higher price → more emotional investment and skin in the game from clients → clients comply more and get better results → better results = more proof and higher perceived likelihood → attracts better clients who are easier to serve → higher margin funds better fulfilment, more attention, real guarantees → value rises further, justifying the price. Lower prices spin the same cycle in reverse (worse clients, worse results, worse proof, thinner service). "Charge the most money and provide the most value" are the same strategy, not a trade-off.
- **Premium positioning target:** not slightly above market; so far above that the buyer thinks "something entirely different must be going on here." That reaction is the category-of-one signal. (Dan Kennedy line he cites: there's no strategic benefit to being second-cheapest, but there is to being the most expensive.)
- **99% rule:** 99% of businesses need to RAISE prices to grow. Profit is oxygen.
- **Discounting doctrine:** never discount the core offer; it teaches customers prices are negotiable. To close a wobbling deal, add a bonus that neutralises their specific objection instead ("fair enough?"). The single sanctioned discount-shaped move: announcing a price RISE with a deadline ("price goes up on the 1st, lock in now"), which cleans the pipeline from a position of strength.
- **Procedure for a small operator raising price:** (1) build the Grand Slam bundle first so the value story exists; (2) announce the rise to the pipeline with a real deadline; (3) raise for new customers first; (4) use the extra margin to visibly improve delivery (this is what makes the cycle virtuous rather than just expensive).

**Why it works:** price is a value signal and a results input, not just revenue; margin is what funds being genuinely better.

### A6. Guarantee selection framework (risk reversal)

Reversing risk is the single highest-leverage conversion lever on an existing offer; craft the guarantee with as much care as the deliverables. **Structure of a strong guarantee:** "If you don't get X result in Y time, we will Z." Without the "or what" (the Z), it's decoration.

**Four types and when each fits:**
1. **Unconditional** (full money-back, no questions): strongest converter; effectively a paid trial. Fits low-ticket B2C, digital/low-marginal-cost products, and businesses with strong fulfilment confidence. Risk: attracts some buyers who never intended to do the work.
2. **Conditional** ("do these steps; if no result, we [refund / work free until / pay for a competitor]"): fits high-ticket and B2B; the conditions should be the exact actions the client must take to succeed anyway (turns the guarantee into a compliance tool). Aim for "better than money back" (e.g., refund + keep the bonuses, or we keep working free until you get there), because a bigger promise deserves a bigger backstop.
3. **Anti-guarantee** ("all sales final", with a strong stated reason why): fits offers exposing valuable IP or one-shot consumables (swipe files, courses that can be binged, custom work). The credibility comes from the reason: "you'll see everything in the first hour, so all sales are final."
4. **Implied** (performance-based: rev-share, pay-per-result, pay-on-show): fits services with measurable outcomes and operators confident in delivery; "if I don't perform, I don't get paid" is the ultimate likelihood signal.

**Decision rules:**
- High fulfilment cost per customer → conditional or anti-guarantee (a refund costs you twice).
- Lower ticket, consumer → broader/unconditional (few bother to claim; conversion lift outweighs refunds).
- Higher ticket, business buyer → specific conditional, tuned to their biggest named fear.
- **Design procedure:** write down the client's biggest fear about paying you ("what do they most dread happening after they pay?"), then invert that exact fear into the guarantee. Name the guarantee something vivid, never "satisfaction guaranteed."
- Guarantees can be **stacked** (e.g., unconditional 30-day + conditional 12-month outcome guarantee).
- **Warning he attaches:** guarantee-motivated buyers can be your worst customers; a guarantee amplifies a good offer, it cannot rescue a bad one.

**Why it works:** Perceived Likelihood is the variable buyers are silently stuck on; a guarantee with teeth transfers the risk from buyer to seller, and only sellers who actually deliver can afford it, so it doubles as a credible quality signal.

### A7. Bonus stacking (the "but wait, there's more" engine)

Doctrine: a single bundled offer is LESS persuasive than the same offer decomposed into a core plus named bonuses. Anchor price to the core, then stack bonuses until the price-to-value gap "snaps the rubber band."

**Rules of the stack (his checklist, condensed):**
1. Always offer bonuses.
2. Each bonus gets a name with a benefit in it (use MAGIC on sub-items too).
3. For each: say how it relates to their problem, what it is, how you built/discovered it, and how it improves their life.
4. Attach proof and an explicit price tag with justification.
5. Tools, templates, and checklists beat "more training" (lower buyer effort = higher value; the value equation governs bonuses too).
6. Each bonus should kill one specific "reason I won't succeed" in the prospect's head, ideally solving their NEXT problem before they hit it.
7. Total stated bonus value should eclipse the core offer's price.
8. Add scarcity/urgency to individual bonuses ("only with this program, never sold separately"; "sign up today and I'll add X, because I reward action takers").
- **Sales sequencing:** one-on-one, ask for the sale FIRST. If yes → reveal bonuses after (wow + reinforces decision). If no → present the bonus matching their objection, ask "fair enough?", ask again.
- **Advanced (free to build):** source bonuses from adjacent businesses; negotiate a group discount for your customers plus a referral commission for you. High perceived value, zero cost, and a side revenue stream.
- **Asset habit:** record every workshop/webinar/call once; each becomes a permanent bonus. Anything expensive to create once but free to give forever is perfect bonus material.

**Why it works:** each named bonus widens the perceived price-to-value gap without discounting, and pre-answers objections one at a time.

### A8. Scarcity playbook (supply-side demand lever)

Scarcity is a function of **quantity**. People want what they can't have, what others want, and what few can access.

**Three legitimate types:** (1) limited seats/slots (total, or per period); (2) limited supply of bonuses; (3) "never available again" editions.
**Small-operator formats:** total servicing cap ("we only hold 25 maintenance clients, 2 spots open"); growth-rate cap ("we take 5 new patients a week; 3 are gone and I have 7 calls booked"); cohort cap ("doors open quarterly"); limited releases for physical product (monthly flavour/design drops).
**Operating rules:** the scarcity must be TRUE; always sell out (set the cap below what you think you can sell), and then tell everyone you sold out (social proof that compounds each cycle); scarcity works hardest on high-ticket, high-access offers (workshops, 1:1, consulting days).

**Why it works:** constrained supply plus visible demand moves buyers from "someday" to "now" and supports premium prices without changing the product.

### A9. Urgency playbook (time-side demand lever)

Urgency is a function of **time**. Deadlines drive decisions. Four ethical, repeatable mechanisms:
1. **Rolling cohorts:** "sign up today, you start with Monday's group; otherwise you wait for the next one." Works even with unlimited actual capacity because the start date is real.
2. **Rolling seasonal promotions:** same core offer, renamed by season/event with a real start and end date and a real countdown ("Summer Booking Window closes Friday").
3. **Pricing/bonus deadlines:** the bonus or current price expires, not the offer. Includes the sanctioned price-rise announcement (A5).
4. **Exploding opportunity:** the opportunity itself decays with time (first-mover niches, limited arbitrage, "each week you wait costs you X in lost billings").
**Rule:** deadlines must be real and enforced; fake countdowns destroy the trust that Perceived Likelihood depends on.

**Why it works:** urgency doesn't add value; it lowers the action threshold so already-convinced buyers stop deferring.

### A10. MAGIC naming formula (the wrapper)

The name of the offer determines ad response, cold outreach reply rate, and organic interest, before anyone reads a word of detail. "Free Six-Week Stress-Release Challenge" outpulls "Float Tank Session" for the identical service.

**M-A-G-I-C components (use 3-5 of them, not always all):**
- **M**agnetic reason why: the hook for the promotion (Free, Spring, Grand Opening, Anniversary, New Year).
- **A**vatar: call out exactly who it's for; the more local/specific, the better ("Brunswick Tradie…", "Salon Owners…").
- **G**oal: the dream outcome in their words (Pain-Free, Fully Booked, First 10 Clients, Double Your Profit).
- **I**nterval: the timeframe (21-Day, 6-Week, 3-Month), which also sets the time-delay expectation.
- **C**ontainer: the word that says "this is a system, not a commodity" (Challenge, Blueprint, Bootcamp, Accelerator, Intensive, Sprint, System, Masterclass).
- Extras: rhyme/alliteration if natural, never forced; name the bonuses and sub-items with the same formula.
**Offer-fatigue refresh order** (when response decays, change in this order, cheapest first): 1. ad creative/images → 2. body copy → 3. headline/name (the "wrapper") → 4. duration → 5. enhancer (the free/discount component) → 6. the monetisation structure itself. You are usually changing the wrapping paper, not the gift; the same offer renamed is "new" to the market.

**Why it works:** the name is the highest-leverage sentence in the business; renaming is a near-zero-cost lead-flow reset that most owners never think to pull.

### A11. Commodity escape (the meta-frame that ties it together)

Diagnosis: if a prospect can think "these are pretty much the same, I'll take the cheaper one," you are commoditised, and price-driven purchasing is a race to the bottom. Same fulfilment + Grand Slam packaging = different product in the buyer's mind. The compounding math he cites: same ad spend, but more respond (~2.5x), more of those buy, and at higher prices; the three multiply. **Test:** put your offer next to your nearest competitor's, strip both logos. If a stranger can't tell the difference in 10 seconds, you're a commodity; run A4.

**Why it works:** growth compounds across response rate × close rate × price; the offer is the only asset that moves all three simultaneously.

## B. DIAGNOSTIC QUESTIONS & HEURISTICS (in his order)

**Order of operations: Market → Niche → Price → Value (equation) → Build (5 steps) → Enhance (scarcity, urgency, bonuses, guarantees) → Name.** He diagnoses in this order and so should BOS.

1. "Do you have a starving crowd?" Is the pain massive, can they pay, can you find them, is the market growing? (Any no → fix avatar first.)
2. "Who exactly is it for?" Can you finish "I help [specific person] get [specific outcome] in [timeframe] without [their biggest sacrifice]"? If the avatar is "anyone who…", niche slap.
3. "Have you actually tried enough offers in this niche, or are you about to blame the market?" (One failed offer is not market feedback.)
4. "Are you priced off competitors or off value?" Did the price come from averaging the market and shaving 10%? That's the broke-competitor copy loop.
5. "What are they REALLY buying?" The vacation or the plane flight? Status in whose eyes?
6. "Why would they believe it will work *for them*?" What proof, what mechanism, what guarantee? (Likelihood is usually the weakest variable in a small operator's offer.)
7. "How fast do they feel the first win?" What happens in the first 48 hours after paying?
8. "What do they still have to do themselves?" List every step the buyer performs; each one is denominator drag.
9. "What problem is left unsolved?" Walk their timeline before/during/after; one unsolved problem = the reason they say no.
10. "If they paid you 10x, what would you do for them? If 1/10th, how would you deliver?" (Reveals the premium tier and the scalable asset respectively.)
11. "What's their biggest fear about paying you?" Invert it: that's the guarantee.
12. "Why should they buy TODAY?" If there's no true scarcity or deadline, expect deferral, not rejection.
13. "Would anyone feel stupid saying no to this?" The final gut-check; if the honest answer is "it's reasonable," it's a commodity.

**Rules of thumb:** starving crowd > offer > persuasion • the pain is the pitch • perception is reality (all four variables are perceived) • fast beats free • done-for-you outprices DIY for the same outcome • never discount the core, add bonuses • bonus value should eclipse core price • sell out on purpose, then say so • deadlines drive decisions • tools/checklists beat more training • under $10M/yr: niche harder • a single client win should pay for the offer many times over • charge the most, deliver the most, same strategy.

## F. GAPS

- **Acquisition.com course video content is gated behind a lead form**; module structure and checklist PDFs verified but not transcript-level detail. The book-derived summaries substitute well since the course tracks the book.
- **The niching price-ladder example varies across retellings** ($19 generic → $1,997 hyper-niched; intermediate rungs and exact avatar labels differ between sources). The magnitude (~100x) and mechanism are consistent; treat exact figures as illustrative.
- **The "2.5x response" figure** in the commodity-escape math appears in the book's worked example; could not be independently verified as a general claim. Present as illustrative compounding, not a benchmark.
- **Guarantee legal caveats:** Hormozi's guarantee doctrine is US-centric. For BOS's Australian audience, Australian Consumer Law mandates certain remedies regardless of stated guarantees; anti-guarantees ("all sales final") can be unlawful for consumer purchases in AU. The doctrine file should flag this; not covered by any source consulted.
